# S8 — Industry Evolution and Strategic Change

## 1. Learning objectives
- Use SWOT to link internal and external analysis.
- Describe the four stages of the industry life cycle, their key success factors and the forces behind them.
- Explain dominant design, the shift from product to process innovation, and shakeouts.
- Identify the KSFs and strategies for mature industries.
- Explain the causes of industry decline, exit barriers, and the four strategies for declining industries.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **SWOT** | A tool linking **internal** strengths and weaknesses (the firm) with **external** opportunities and threats (the industry). `[PN][SD]` |
| **Industry evolution** | How an industry's demand, technology, structure and KSFs change over time. `[PN]` |
| **Industry life cycle** | Introduction → growth → maturity → decline. Sales follow an S-curve. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Market penetration (rate)** | The share of potential buyers who already own the product. ⚠ This is not penetration *pricing*. `[TB]` |
| **Dominant design** | A product architecture that defines the look, functionality and production methods of a product and becomes accepted by the whole industry. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Shakeout** | A phase when growth slows and weaker competitors exit or are acquired, so the industry consolidates. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Strategic innovation** | Changing how a firm creates, delivers or captures value: business-model-level innovation. `[PN]` |
| **Exit barriers** | Costs or constraints that keep firms in an industry even when profits are low. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Harvest** | Maximising cash flow while progressively cutting investment. `[PN][SD]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 SWOT `[PN][SD]`
| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal (firm)** | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| **External (industry)** | Opportunities | Threats |

**Lecture example: Patagonia** `[PN]`
- **Strengths:** brand reputation, sustainability initiatives, material innovation, strong culture, philanthropy.
- **Weaknesses:** high prices, limited categories, high innovation costs, difficulty scaling sustainable practices.
- **Opportunities:** new products, partnerships, emerging markets, e-commerce, community engagement.
- **Threats:** competition, supply-chain disruption, changing preferences, economic fluctuations, regulation.

**Exam tip:** don't just list factors. Explain the *strategic implication*, for example using strengths to exploit opportunities.

### 3.2 Two forces driving evolution `[PN][SD]`
1. **Demand growth:** market penetration and the S-curve.
2. **Production and diffusion of knowledge:** technology, standards and customer knowledge.

### 3.3 The four stages `[PN][SD]`
| Stage | Demand | Technology / innovation | Competition | KSF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Introduction** | Low, few customers, product unknown | Rapid **product** innovation, competing technologies | Few firms | Product innovation |
| **Growth** | Rapidly increasing, customers better informed | Dominant design emerges → **process** innovation | Many entrants | Design for manufacture, process innovation, scaling capacity |
| **Maturity** | Saturated, mainly replacement demand; price-sensitive, knowledgeable buyers | Standardised, incremental innovation | Intense, price-based; excess capacity; foreign competition | Cost efficiency |
| **Decline** | Falling; shift to substitutes | Little innovation within the industry; innovation elsewhere replaces it | Price wars, exits, consolidation | Low overheads, rationalisation, customer selection |

The 2024–25 notes also name an **intermediate stage (shakeout)** between growth and maturity: sales growth slows, focus shifts to cost reduction, and firms merge or exit. `[SD]`

### 3.4 Knowledge, dominant design and innovation `[PN][SD]`
- **Introduction:** many competing technologies; customers know little.
- **Transition to growth:** customers learn, standards emerge, and a **dominant design** appears.
- After the dominant design, innovation shifts **from product to process** (efficiency), then becomes incremental in maturity.

### 3.5 Patterns of evolution: the model is not destiny `[PN][SD]`
- **Technology-intensive industries** (pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, computers) may keep emerging-industry features for a long time.
- **Basic-necessity industries** (food processing, construction, apparel) reach maturity but do not decline.
- **Life-cycle regeneration:** motorcycles, televisions.
- **Durations vary greatly:**
  - US railroads: first railroad in 1827, decline phase by the 1950s.
  - MP3 players: introduced 1997, growth with the iPod from 2001, decline from 2008. `[SD]`

### 3.6 Industry evolution examples `[PN]`
- **DVDs:**
  - introduced in 1997;
  - grew by replacing VHS;
  - matured (about $16.3bn in the lecture's figure);
  - declined with streaming (Netflix, Disney+).
  - This is substitution from new technology.
- **Digital cameras:** sales rose, then collapsed because of smartphone cameras. This is innovation in *another* industry.

### 3.7 Competing in mature industries `[PN][SD]`
**Features:**
- saturation and replacement demand;
- slow growth;
- price competition;
- standardised technology;
- limited scope for differentiation;
- international competition.

As a result, opportunities shift **from differentiation-based to cost-based** factors. Maturity does **not** mean no opportunity or no innovation.

**Four KSFs / strategic approaches:**
1. **New sources of cost advantage:** scale, low-cost inputs, low overheads, efficiency.
2. **New segments and customers:** attractive niches inside unattractive industries.
   - Harley-Davidson in motorcycles; Nintendo Wii in consoles.
   - LaEsse grew by locating stores in small and medium towns `[SD]`.
3. **New sources of differentiation:**
   - brand, complementary services;
   - **augmenting, bundling and theming** (Hard Rock Café; Ferrero/Barilla line extensions `[SD]`);
   - **customised solutions** (Alstom trains, Evaneos travel).
4. **New sources of innovation, or strategic innovation:**
   - breaking out of the "maturity mindset";
   - Hilti (servitisation), Timberland (rental), Nespresso (home coffee systems), Virgin (cruises).

This conflicts with S19, where offensive strategies **take market share from rivals** and defensive strategies **reduce the probability and impact of attacks**. ### 3.8 Competing in declining industries `[PN][SD]`
**Causes:** changes in consumer lifestyles, new technologies, demographic trends.

**Features:** excess capacity, little technological change, consolidation (with some new entry as others exit), ageing equipment and workforce, exits.

**Smooth capacity adjustment depends on:**
1. the **predictability** of decline;
2. **exit barriers:** durable, specialised assets, closure costs, managerial commitment;
3. the **strategies of surviving firms**.

High exit barriers keep unprofitable firms in the market, which intensifies price competition.

**Four strategies** `[PN][SD]`:
| Strategy | Objective | Typical actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Leadership** | Become the dominant firm as others leave | Encourage rivals to exit, acquire rivals or their capacity, show commitment, dispel optimism about the industry's future, raise the stakes |
| **Niche** | Dominate a segment where demand persists | Focus on profitable customer groups, defend that position |
| **Harvest** | Controlled disinvestment that maximises cash | Cut investment and unnecessary costs, exploit strengths |
| **Divest (quickly)** | Exit while assets still have value | Sell the business or assets early in the decline |

**Choosing a strategy** (Grant's matrix) `[PN][TB]` ⚠ labels to verify:
| | Strong competitive position | Weak competitive position |
|---|---|---|
| **Industry structure favourable to decline** (orderly, low exit barriers) | Leadership or niche | Harvest or divest quickly |
| **Industry structure unfavourable** (price wars, high exit barriers) | Niche or harvest | Divest quickly |

Decline does not always mean immediate exit: strong firms can benefit as competitors leave.

## 4. Case 4 — The COVID testing industry `[PN]` + `[REC]`
An unusually **compressed life cycle**:
| Stage | Period (per your notes) | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | "2019" ⚠ (testing actually began early **2020**) | Limited capacity, rapid PCR test development, emergency regulatory authorisations |
| Growth | 2020–2021 | Demand explodes, capacity expands, new suppliers enter, investment surges |
| Maturity | 2021–2023 | Standardised tests (including rapid antigen tests), falling prices, competition on convenience and speed |
| Decline | From 2023 | Vaccination, immunity and policy changes cut demand; excess capacity; consolidation and price pressure |

**Link to theory** `[REC]`:
- **Introduction:** product innovation and competing technologies (PCR vs antigen).
- **Growth:** a dominant design emerges (the lateral-flow antigen format) and the focus shifts to process innovation and scale.
- **Maturity:** cost efficiency becomes the KSF.
- **Decline:** the predictability of decline, exit barriers (dedicated capacity), and the choice between harvest, niche (for example, multiplex respiratory tests) and divest.

**Lesson:** external shocks can accelerate the whole life cycle into a few years.

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Product innovation: dominates introduction | Process innovation: dominates growth (after the dominant design) |
| Growth: entry, capacity expansion | Shakeout: slowdown, exits, M&A |
| Mature KSF: cost efficiency | Introduction KSF: product innovation |
| Harvest: stay, cut investment, milk cash | Divest: sell and leave early |
| Leadership: buy the remaining market | Niche: dominate a surviving segment |
| Strengths/weaknesses: internal | Opportunities/threats: external |

## 7. Exam traps
- **Maturity does not mean no innovation.** Strategic innovation is a mature-industry strategy.
- The dominant design marks the shift **to process** innovation, not to product innovation.
- High **exit** barriers make decline **worse**, not better.
- Not every industry declines: food processing and construction stay mature.
- Harvest ≠ divest: harvest stays in the industry while reducing investment.
- In SWOT, "growing e-commerce" is an **opportunity** (external), not a strength.

## 9. Memorise this
- **Drivers:** demand growth + knowledge diffusion.
- **Introduction** = product innovation · **Growth** = process innovation · **Maturity** = cost efficiency · **Decline** = rationalisation.
- **Dominant design → product-to-process shift → shakeout.**
- **Mature strategies:** cost · new segments · new differentiation · strategic innovation.
- **Decline:** leadership · niche · harvest · divest. These depend on competitive position × industry structure.
- **Exit barriers keep weak firms in → more rivalry.**
