# S11 — Technology and Competitive Advantage

## 1. Learning objectives
- Distinguish invention from innovation and describe the development process from knowledge to diffusion.
- Explain appropriability and its four determinants, including intellectual property types.
- Evaluate first-mover vs. follower strategies.
- Explain types of standards, network externalities and how to win a standards war.
- Manage technological and market risk, and choose a strategy to exploit innovation.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Invention** | The creation of new products or processes through new knowledge or new combinations of existing knowledge. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Innovation** | The initial **commercialisation** of an invention: turning a prototype into a market-ready product or process. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Diffusion** | The spread of an innovation: **adoption** by customers (demand side) and **imitation** by competitors (supply side). `[PN][SD]` |
| **Appropriability** | The innovator's ability to capture the economic value its innovation creates. `[PN]` |
| **Tacitness** | How hard the knowledge is to articulate, understand and copy. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Complementary resources** | The assets needed to commercialise an innovation: manufacturing, distribution, marketing, finance, service, complementary technologies. `[PN][SD]` |
| **First-mover advantage** | The benefits of entering before competitors. `[PN]` |
| **Standard** | A format, interface or specification that ensures compatibility and that firms in the industry must meet. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Network externalities** | A product becomes more valuable as more people use it. `[PN]` |
| **Real options** | Making small initial investments while keeping the flexibility to expand, delay or abandon. `[PN]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 From knowledge to diffusion `[PN][SD]`
**Basic knowledge → invention → innovation → diffusion** (adoption + imitation)

- An innovation needs a real market need and a functioning supply and demand network.
  - Counter-example `[SD]`: Meta's demo of remote surgery in the metaverse, with no evident need.
  - AI is arguably still being diffused to the mass market `[SD]`.
- **Moving from invention to innovation** means balancing creativity with discipline and integrating functions. Methods `[PN]`: cross-functional teams, product champions, buying innovation, open innovation, corporate incubators.

### 3.2 Who captures the value? Appropriability `[PN][SD]`
Value may go to innovators, customers, suppliers or **imitators**. Four factors decide how much the innovator keeps:

| Factor | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Property rights** | Legal protection: patents, copyright, trademarks, trade secrets |
| **Tacitness and complexity** | Hard-to-codify, complex knowledge is harder to copy |
| **Lead time** | The head start over followers before imitation |
| **Complementary resources** | Owning the assets needed to commercialise lets the innovator capture the value |

**Intellectual property rights** `[PN][SD]`:
- **Patents:** exclusive rights to an invention (product, process, design). ⚠ The 2024–25 notes say 17 years; the current standard term is **20 years from filing**. `[TB]`
- **Copyright:** artistic and creative works.
- **Trademarks:** names, symbols and brands.
- **Trade secrets:** recipes, formulas, processes, customer lists.

Example of complementary resources: apps make a smartphone valuable `[SD]`. An innovative smartphone without manufacturing, distribution and marketing fails `[PN]`.

**Key point:** inventing a technology does **not** guarantee profit from it.

### 3.3 First mover vs. follower `[PN][SD]`
Three factors decide whether leading or following pays:
1. **Protection** of the innovation (IP, lead time). Strong protection favours first movers.
2. **The importance of complementary resources.** First movers may have to build the infrastructure themselves. Followers benefit from it, and firms that already own complementary resources can afford to wait.
3. **The potential to set a standard.** If the technology can become *the* standard, being first matters.

| First movers | Followers |
|---|---|
| May set the standard | Learn from pioneers' mistakes |
| Benefit from lead time | Avoid initial development costs |
| Face technological and market uncertainty | Exploit established infrastructure |

**Followers that won** `[PN]`: Apple iPod (after earlier MP3 players), Amazon Kindle (after Sony's e-reader), Microsoft Internet Explorer (after Netscape).

**Leaders that won** `[SD]`: Toyota hybrids (time to perfect the technology and lower costs); the iPhone defined the "smartphone".

**Low-cost followers** `[SD]`: Xiaomi and Huawei had lower R&D costs by following.

### 3.4 Standards `[PN][SD]`
| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Public / open** | Available to all | 5G (3G in the 2024–25 notes) |
| **Private / proprietary** | Controlled by a firm | Adobe PDF |
| **Mandatory** | Imposed by law or regulation | Animal-welfare standards |
| **De facto** | Emerges through market adoption | Microsoft Windows |

Other examples: Intel x86, VHS, Java, Blu-ray. Sony used exclusive Blu-ray film releases to win against **HD DVD** (Toshiba). `[SD]`

**Why standards matter:** they drive adoption and compatibility, and the firm that controls the standard can earn large returns.

### 3.5 Winning a standards war `[PN][SD]`
**Four strategies:**
1. **Assess network externalities.** Will a standard emerge? Does value rise with users?
2. **Assemble allies:** customers, complementors, even competitors (build a bandwagon).
3. **Pre-empt the market:** early launch, pre-launch publicity, promotion.
4. **Manage expectations:** convince the market you will win, and keep your promises.

**Key resources needed:**
- an installed customer base;
- intellectual property;
- the ability to keep innovating;
- first-mover advantage;
- strength in complements;
- reputation and brand.

### 3.6 Managing risk `[PN]`
- **Technological uncertainty:** which technology will win?
- **Market uncertainty:** will customers adopt it?

**Strategies:**
1. **Cooperate with lead users** (advanced customers who give early feedback).
2. **Limit risk exposure:** avoid large irreversible investments; lease, outsource, form alliances, keep debt low.
3. **Stay flexible:** keep options open (**real options**), respond quickly to new information, learn from mistakes.

### 3.7 Strategies to exploit innovation `[PN]`
Ordered from least to most commitment:
| Strategy | What it is | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Licensing** | Another firm uses the innovation for royalties | Low investment, low risk | Limited returns; imitation risk |
| **Outsourcing** certain functions | External firms perform selected activities | Access to external capabilities | Dependence on suppliers |
| **Strategic alliance** | Cooperation with another firm | Shared resources, flexibility | Coordination and information-sharing risks |
| **Joint venture** | A jointly owned business | Shared investment and risk | Potential disagreements |
| **Internal commercialisation** | Develop, produce and market alone | Full control, highest potential returns | Highest investment and risk |

**Choice depends on:** financial resources, manufacturing and distribution capabilities, the desired level of control, risk, and access to complementary resources.
- **Licensing** suits valuable technology with limited resources.
- **Internal commercialisation** suits a firm with enough resources that wants control.

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Invention (new knowledge) | Innovation (commercialisation) |
| Adoption (customers) | Imitation (competitors) |
| Open standard | Proprietary standard |
| De facto (market) | Mandatory (law) |
| Technological uncertainty | Market uncertainty |
| Licensing (low control, low risk) | Internal commercialisation (high control, high risk) |
| Network externalities (demand side) | Learning effects (supply-side cost reductions) |

## 7. Exam traps
- **First mover ≠ always the winner.** It depends on protection, complementary resources and the potential to set a standard.
- **A de facto standard** comes from market acceptance, **not** regulation.
- Owning complementary resources can let a firm **wait** and still win as a follower.
- Licensing is the *least* risky way to commercialise but captures the *least* value.
- Innovation is the *commercialisation*, not the invention.
