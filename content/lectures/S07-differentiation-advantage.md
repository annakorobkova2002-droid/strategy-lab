# S7 — Competitive Advantage II: Differentiation and Generic Strategies

## 1. Learning objectives
- Define differentiation advantage and distinguish tangible from intangible differentiation.
- Distinguish differentiation from segmentation (broad vs. focused differentiation).
- Analyse differentiation from the demand side and the supply side (the nine drivers of uniqueness).
- Explain functions vs. signals, and search vs. experience goods.
- Explain Porter's four generic strategies and "stuck in the middle".
- Apply the concepts to Chantecler.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Differentiation** (Porter) | Providing something **unique** that is **valuable to buyers** beyond simply offering a low price. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Tangible differentiation** | Observable product characteristics: size, colour, materials, performance, packaging, complementary services. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Intangible differentiation** | Unobservable, subjective characteristics that appeal to image, status, identity and exclusivity. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Total customer responsiveness** | Differentiation covers the whole relationship between firm and customer, not only the product. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Signals** | Features that **communicate** quality or value (brand, packaging, advertising, premises). `[PN]` |
| **Search goods** | Goods whose quality can be judged *before* purchase from external characteristics. `[SD]` |
| **Experience goods** | Goods whose quality can only be judged *after* consumption. `[SD]` |
| **Stuck in the middle** | Failing to achieve a clear advantage through either low cost or differentiation. `[PN]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

| Happy Socks | Patterns, self-expression |

### 3.2 Differentiation vs. segmentation `[PN][SD]`
- **Differentiation = HOW** a firm distinguishes its offering.
- **Segmentation = WHERE**: which customers, needs and locations it targets.

Differentiation does **not necessarily** imply segmentation:
- **Broad-scope differentiation** appeals to what customers share. Examples: McDonald's, Honda, Gillette.
- **Focused differentiation** appeals to what distinguishes a customer group. Examples: Harley-Davidson, Armani; MTV in the 2024–25 notes.

### 3.3 Demand side: understanding customers `[PN]`
Identify:
- customer needs and preferences;
- the attributes that matter;
- purchase criteria and motivations;
- willingness to pay a premium;
- demographic, social and psychological characteristics.

**Four steps in formulating a differentiation strategy:**
1. Choose a product positioning based on the relevant attributes.
2. Select the target customer group.
3. Ensure the product is compatible with customer needs.
4. Evaluate the costs and benefits of differentiating.

### 3.4 Supply side: the nine drivers of uniqueness `[PN][SD]`
1. Product features and performance.
2. Complementary services (credit, delivery, repair; for example, AirPods with an iPhone).
3. Intensity of marketing activities.
4. Technology embodied in design and manufacture.
5. Quality of purchased inputs.
6. Procedures (quality control, service standards, frequency of sales visits).
7. Skill and experience of employees.
8. Location.
9. Degree of vertical integration.

**Demand side = what customers value. Supply side = how the firm can deliver uniqueness.**

### 3.5 Functions and signals `[PN][SD]`
- **Functions:** the real characteristics and performance of the offering.
- **Signals:** the cues that communicate it. Examples:
  - perfume: brand, packaging, advertising;
  - financial services: professional staff, conservative offices.

**The harder performance is to judge before purchase (experience goods), the more important signalling becomes.**

### 3.6 Differentiation along the value chain `[PN]`
| Activity | Differentiation opportunity |
|---|---|
| Inbound logistics | High-quality materials and components |
| Operations | Defect-free products, variety |
| Outbound logistics | Fast delivery, efficient order processing |
| Marketing & sales | Strong brand reputation |
| Service | Technical support, financing, spare parts |
| HRM | Training for customer service |
| Technology development | Unique features, rapid product development |
| Firm infrastructure | Information systems that support rapid response |

### 3.7 Does differentiation mean higher prices? `[PN][SD]`
Not necessarily. Differentiation may instead aim at loyalty, preference or protecting profitability.

**Profit per unit = price − unit cost.** Differentiation pays only if the **price premium exceeds the extra cost** of differentiating.

The lecture's price–cost–profit diagram (slide 18) compares an average firm, a differentiator, a strong differentiator, and a commodity differentiator with low costs.

Example: an iPhone costs a few hundred euros to produce but sells at a large margin. `[SD]`

### 3.8 Cost vs. differentiation: requirements `[PN]`
| | Cost leadership | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Key strategy elements | Scale, process innovation, cost control, efficiency | Branding, advertising, product innovation, quality, design |
| Resources and capabilities | Access to capital, process engineering skills | Marketing, product engineering, creativity, research |
| Organisation | Specialised jobs, strict cost control, **quantitative** targets | Cross-functional coordination, **qualitative** targets |

### 3.9 Porter's generic strategies `[PN]` (slide 23)
| Competitive scope | Lower cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad market** | **Cost leadership**: Walmart, Aldi, McDonald's, Dell | **Differentiation**: Nike, Apple, Mercedes, Marriott |
| **Narrow market** | **Cost focus**: Basko (Ligurian grocer) | **Differentiation focus**: Tesla premium EVs, Lush, Omega, La Compagnie |

**Choosing a strategy:** combine market and competitive analysis with internal resources and capabilities, and customer needs. The firm's strategy, resources and environment must fit.

**Potential problems:**
- rivals imitate cost practices;
- differentiation becomes too expensive;
- customer perceptions shift;
- it is hard to combine efficiency and uniqueness;
- new business models make existing strategies obsolete.

### 3.10 Stuck in the middle `[PN]`
The firm cannot compete on price and does not offer enough uniqueness to justify its prices. It lacks a coherent strategy.

**Nuance from the lecture:** combining low cost and differentiation is *not* automatically a failure. It is increasingly important but hard and costly. Blue Ocean's **value innovation** in S9–10 is exactly this combination.

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Cost advantage: lower cost for a similar product | Differentiation: uniqueness that customers pay for |
| Differentiation: HOW | Segmentation: WHERE |
| Broad differentiation: shared needs (Honda) | Focused differentiation: group-specific needs (Harley) |
| Tangible (observable) | Intangible (image, status) |
| Function (what it does) | Signal (what it communicates) |
| Search good (judge before buying) | Experience good (judge after; signals matter) |
| Cost leadership (broad, low cost) | Cost focus (narrow, low cost) |
| Quantitative targets (cost) | Qualitative targets (differentiation) |

## 7. Guest speaker — Chantecler (Vittoria Carandini) `[PN]` (based on the presentation)
**Origins:**
- Capri; the figure of Pietro Capuano, "Chantecler".
- After Allied forces arrived in 1944, he conceived the **Bell of Saint Michael** as a symbol of peace, luck and friendship.
- It later became the **Campanella** jewel (around 1990 according to the presentation).

**Value creation:**
- local symbol → emotionally meaningful jewel → recognisable design → evolving collection;
- the shape stays recognisable while materials, colours and designs change, giving variety and collectability.

**Craftsmanship:**
- Made in Italy, production in **Valenza**;
- traditional techniques (enamel, engraving, stone setting, hand-shaped coral and turquoise) combined with 3D modelling and micro-casting.
- Heritage is **reinterpreted**, not merely reproduced.

**Product architecture:**
- **High Jewelry** at the top;
- **Prêt-à-porter** and **Icons** in the middle;
- the silver collection **Et Voilà** at the base, presented as the same design language for everyday wear (not as "entry level").

**Positioning:**
- luxury as **individual identity and uniqueness** rather than conspicuous status;
- "*Tu sei un'isola*".

**Storytelling:** the *Capritude* magazine, events and branded gifts extend the experience beyond the purchase.

**Linking to theory** `[REC]`:
- **Intangible differentiation** through story, symbol and identity.
- **Signals:** jewellery has experience-good and status characteristics, so storytelling and brand cues matter.
- **Drivers of uniqueness:** input quality, employee skill (artisans), location (Capri/Valenza), and control over production.
- **VRIO:** heritage and the Campanella story are valuable, rare and hard to imitate (path dependence).
- **Generic strategy:** differentiation focus.

**One-sentence summary** `[PN]`: Chantecler uses Capri heritage, the Campanella symbol, Italian craftsmanship and consistent storytelling to create a recognisable luxury brand whose value extends beyond its materials.

## 8. Practical application
For a differentiation question:
1. Name the type of differentiation (tangible or intangible).
2. Identify the scope (broad or focused).
3. Cover the demand side (what customers value) and the supply side (which drivers deliver it).
4. Say whether the firm relies on signals.
5. Do the price–cost check.
6. Place the firm in the generic matrix.
7. Assess the risk of being stuck in the middle.

## 9. Exam traps
- Differentiation **does not** automatically mean a higher price, nor does it require segmentation.
- **Cost focus ≠ cost leadership:** the difference is scope, not cost level.
- Signals matter **more** for experience goods, not search goods.
- Combining cost and differentiation is not *automatically* stuck in the middle.
- Differentiation can come from **any** value-chain activity, not only product design.
