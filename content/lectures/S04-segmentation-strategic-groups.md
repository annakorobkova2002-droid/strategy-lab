# S4 — Segmentation and Strategic Groups

## 1. Learning objectives
- Explain why an industry may need to be analysed at a more disaggregated level.
- Run a segmentation analysis in its five phases.
- Identify and map strategic groups, and explain mobility barriers.
- Distinguish broad from narrow strategic positioning.
- Apply segmentation to the MyStyleBags guest case.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Market segmentation** | Dividing a market into groups of customers with similar needs who respond similarly to a marketing action. `[SD]` (paraphrasing McDonald & Dunbar, Kerin, Kotler) |
| **Segment** | A homogeneous sub-section of customers that may be selected as a target and reached with a distinct marketing mix. `[SD]` |
| **Segmentation analysis (strategic)** | Using segmentation to identify *which parts* of an industry are attractive and what it takes to succeed in each. `[PN]` |
| **Strategic group** | A group of firms within an industry that follow the same or similar strategies. `[SD]` |
| **Mobility barriers** | Barriers that stop firms moving from one strategic group to another: entry barriers *within* an industry. `[TB]` |
| **Broad (wide) positioning** | Targeting many segments by addressing needs they share. `[SD][PN]` |
| **Narrow positioning** | Targeting a specific group with distinctive needs. `[SD][PN]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Why segment? `[PN][SD]`
Industries are often too broad to analyse as one unit. Different segments can have different:
- customers;
- competitors;
- **Five Forces**;
- **KSFs**;
- **profitability**.

Segmentation solves Five Forces' "level of analysis" limitation from S2.
- **Industry analysis:** is the industry attractive?
- **Segmentation:** *which parts* are attractive?
- **KSFs:** what must the firm do well there? `[PN]`

### 3.2 Segmentation variables `[PN][SD]`
- **Customer characteristics:** price or income, customer type (B2B/B2C), geography, demographics, usage or occasion.
- **Product characteristics:** product attributes, price range, distribution channel.

Example: the world automobile market can be segmented by vehicle size or type, price range and region. `[SD]`

### 3.3 The five phases of segmentation analysis `[SD]`
1. **Identify key variables and categories.**
   - List the possible segmentation variables.
   - Reduce them to the 2–3 most important.
   - Define discrete categories for each.
2. **Build a segmentation matrix.** Combine the variables into a grid of segments.
3. **Evaluate segment attractiveness.** Apply Five Forces to each segment: size, growth, rivalry, buyer power, and so on.
4. **Identify KSFs in each segment.**
5. **Analyse attractiveness for the firm.** Consider:
   - economies of scope across segments;
   - how similar the segments' KSFs are;
   - the benefits of focusing on one segment vs. covering several.

### 3.4 Strategic group analysis `[SD]` + `[TB]`
**Identifying strategic groups:**
1. Find the main strategic variables that distinguish firms. Examples: product range, geographical scope, price/quality, degree of vertical integration, distribution channels.
2. Position each firm on these variables, usually choosing two variables for a 2D map.
3. Identify clusters of firms. Each cluster is a strategic group. On the map, circle size often shows the group's market share.

**Uses** `[TB]`:
- Identify the firm's **closest competitors** (rivalry is usually strongest within a group).
- Explain **profitability differences** between groups.
- Spot **mobility barriers** that protect a group.
- Find **empty spaces** on the map, which may be strategic opportunities. This links to Blue Ocean's "look across strategic groups" path in S9–10.

**Limitations** `[TB]`:
- Results depend on the variables chosen.
- Firms within a group are rarely identical.
- Groups explain profitability differences only partly.

### 3.5 Example strategic group map (illustrative) `[REC]`
European automobile industry. **x-axis:** price/brand positioning (mass → premium → luxury). **y-axis:** geographical scope (regional → global).
- **Group 1:** global mass producers (Volkswagen brand, Toyota).
- **Group 2:** global premium producers (BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Audi).
- **Group 3:** niche luxury or performance producers (Ferrari, Rolls-Royce).
- **Group 4:** regional value brands (Dacia).

Mobility barriers between groups: brand heritage, dealer networks, technology, capital.

### 3.6 Broad vs. narrow positioning `[SD][PN]`
| | Broad (wide) | Narrow |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Many segments | A specific segment or niche |
| Logic | Address the needs segments share | Address distinctive needs precisely |
| Offer | Standardised, fewer variations | Tailored, specialised |
| Example | A car maker selling to families, young professionals and older drivers on safety, comfort and price | A luxury car brand for high-income buyers seeking exclusivity and status |

This prepares Porter's **competitive scope** dimension (broad vs. narrow) in the generic strategies of S7.

## 4. Frameworks
Segmentation matrix (five phases) · strategic group map · mobility barriers · broad vs. narrow positioning.

## 5. Authors and theories
- **Kotler** (2011), **Kerin** (2011), **McDonald & Dunbar** (2004): definitions of segmentation.
- **Hunt** (1972), **Porter** (1980): strategic groups and mobility barriers. `[TB]`
- **Grant & Jordan** ch.2.

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Segment: a group of **customers** | Strategic group: a group of **firms** |
| Segmentation: *where* within the industry demand is attractive | Strategic groups: *who* competes how |
| Entry barriers (into the industry) | Mobility barriers (between groups) |
| Differentiation: *how* to compete (S7) | Segmentation: *where* to compete |

## 7. Guest speaker — MyStyleBags (Leopoldo Orlando) `[PN]` (key ideas from class)
**Positioning:**
- Made in Italy.
- **Personalisation.**
- Premium yet accessible.
- Product quality.
- A wide product range.

**Segmentation variables discussed:**
- gender;
- geography;
- income/price;
- **usage occasion:** personal, travel, gifts, corporate/hospitality.

**Competitive logic:** personalisation → differentiation → higher perceived value → lower price sensitivity.

**Competitors and alternatives mentioned:** RIMOWA, Coccinelle, MC2 Saint Barth, Longchamp.

**Strategic group reading** `[REC]`:
- RIMOWA: premium/luxury travel.
- Longchamp and Coccinelle: accessible premium fashion.
- MC2 Saint Barth: lifestyle resort brand.
- MyStyleBags tries to occupy a **personalised, accessible-premium** position. It also uses B2B gifting and hospitality as a distinct segment.

**Lesson:** industry analysis tells you whether bags are attractive; segmentation tells you which usage and price segments are; KSFs (personalisation, quality, speed) tell you what to do well there.

## 8. Practical application
For any company case:
1. Choose two segmentation variables and draw the matrix.
2. Place the firm and its rivals.
3. Identify the strategic group and its mobility barriers.
4. Judge whether the firm is in an attractive segment, and whether an empty space on the map is worth entering.

## 9. Exam traps
- Strategic groups cluster **firms**; segments cluster **customers**.
- Strategic-group axes should be **strategic variables** (scope, price/quality, integration), not performance outcomes such as profit.
- Differentiation does **not** require segmentation (see broad differentiation in S7).
- Rivalry is usually most intense **within** a strategic group, not between groups.
