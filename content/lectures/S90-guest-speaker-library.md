# Guest Speaker Library

## 1. How to use this library
Each company follows the same nine points: **overview · business model · positioning · challenges · resources & capabilities · frameworks · decisions & implications · exam questions · summary**.

| Type | Guests |
|---|---|
| **From the lecture + research** | MyStyleBags (S4), Chantecler (S7) |

## 2. Velasca — S1 & S21 (Enrico Casati, co-founder)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- A Milan footwear and menswear brand **founded in 2013** by **Enrico Casati and Jacopo Sebastio**, both Bocconi graduates. Jacopo Sebastio is the CEO.
- The name comes from Milan's **Torre Velasca**.
- The idea came from a gap they saw **between designer shoes and fast fashion**: handmade Italian quality at fair prices.
- 2023 revenue was about **€23m**, with EBITDA of about €2.2m. `[REC]`

**2. Business model.**
- **Direct-to-consumer (DTC):** shoes are handmade by Italian artisan workshops and sold under Velasca's own brand, with no wholesalers or department stores in between.
- It started **online-only**, then opened directly operated stores ("botteghe"). By 2019 revenue was split roughly **half online, half physical stores**.
- **Formal shoes cost about €180–250**, well below luxury brands of similar construction.
- **Range extensions:** casual styles, **women's footwear**, then a **full clothing line** ("total look").
- **Next steps:** testing **franchising and selective wholesale**; international flagships in **London, Paris and New York**.

**3. Positioning.**
- **Accessible premium / "accessible luxury"**: craftsmanship and Made in Italy at a fair price.
- In Porter's terms this is **differentiation** on quality, design and heritage storytelling, with a cost edge from cutting out intermediaries.

**4. Main challenges.**
- **Scaling without diluting craftsmanship.** Artisan capacity is limited.
- **Store economics.** Physical stores raise fixed costs compared with pure e-commerce.
- **International expansion** (CAGE: cultural fit of formal Italian shoes, distance to the US).
- **Imitation by other DTC brands**, and the risk of losing brand coherence as the range widens.

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- A **network of artisan workshops** (relationships built over years, hard to imitate).
- **Brand storytelling** around Milan and Made in Italy.
- **First-party customer data** from DTC, used for product and location decisions.
- **Omnichannel retail know-how** (combining online and physical).

**6. Frameworks.**
- **S1:** business strategy (how to compete).
- **S7:** differentiation (tangible quality plus intangible brand), broad vs. focused scope.
- **S14:** the BMC, especially channels and customer relationships.
- **S15:** a **channel king** pattern: disintermediation through a direct channel.
- **S12:** internationalisation choices.
- **S5:** VRIO of the artisan network.

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| DTC instead of wholesale | Higher margin and customer data; but the brand must build awareness itself |
| Opening physical stores | Builds trust and fit (shoes are an *experience good*), but adds fixed costs |
| Women's line + clothing | Larger basket and lifetime value; risk of diluting the "shoemaker" identity |
| Franchising / wholesale tests | Faster, capital-light growth; less control over the experience |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "How does Velasca create competitive advantage?" (Differentiation plus DTC cost logic.)
- "Draw Velasca's Business Model Canvas."
- "Should Velasca move into wholesale? Discuss using the BMC and the risks to its differentiation."

**9. Summary.** **Velasca = Made-in-Italy craftsmanship + accessible premium price + DTC omnichannel model; the challenge is to scale without losing authenticity.**

## 3. BEKO — S1 & S20 (Francesco Misurelli)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- **Beko** is the flagship global brand of **Arçelik**, the Turkish home-appliance group controlled by **Koç Holding**.
- In **April 2024**, Arçelik combined its European business with **Whirlpool's European appliance business**, creating **Beko Europe** (75% Arçelik, 25% Whirlpool).
- At launch it had about **€5.5bn revenue, 11 plants and about 20,000 employees**, making it **Europe's largest home-appliance group** by volume.
- In **June 2026**, Arçelik agreed to buy Whirlpool's remaining 25% for **€71.5m**, taking **full ownership**. The Whirlpool brand licence remains.

**2. Business model.**
- **A multi-brand portfolio:** Beko, Grundig, Blomberg, Arctic, Elektrabregenz, plus former Whirlpool brands such as **Indesit, Hotpoint, Bauknecht, Ignis, Privileg, Polar**, and licensed **Whirlpool/Ariston**.
- Scale manufacturing of washing machines, fridges, ovens and dishwashers, sold through retailers and e-commerce.

**3. Positioning.**
- Beko traditionally plays **value-for-money**: reliable, energy-efficient appliances at mid-market prices.
- The portfolio now spans entry to upper-mid segments (multi-brand segmentation).
- Close to **broad cost leadership with differentiation elements** (energy efficiency, features).

**4. Main challenges.**
- **A mature, price-competitive industry** (S8) with excess European capacity and replacement-driven demand.
- Strong rivals: **Bosch–Siemens (BSH), Electrolux, Samsung, LG, Haier**.
- **Post-merger integration:** Beko Europe has been **closing and resizing plants**, including Italian sites such as a freezer factory in Siena and a washing-machine/dryer facility in the Marche (November 2025). By 2026 it had about **14,000 employees and 16 brands**.
- Rising energy-efficiency and regulatory costs.

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Manufacturing scale** and low-cost production bases (Turkey, Eastern Europe).
- A broad **brand portfolio** with local strength in each country.
- **R&D** in energy-efficient appliances.
- **M&A and integration capability** (Arçelik's acquisition track record).

**6. Frameworks.**
- **S1:** corporate (where: buying Whirlpool Europe) vs. business strategy (how: value for money).
- **S2:** Five Forces of a mature industry.
- **S6:** economies of scale.
- **S8:** mature-industry KSFs and **leadership** strategy (consolidating the industry).
- **S12:** global industry; entry by acquisition and JV.
- **S18:** restructuring (downsizing, plant closures).

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Combining with Whirlpool Europe | Scale, market share, brand portfolio; but overlapping plants must be rationalised |
| Plant closures | Lower costs (short term); loss of human capital and social and political backlash (downsizing risks, S18) |
| Buying the remaining 25% (2026) | Faster decisions and a simpler governance and brand architecture |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Is the European home-appliance industry attractive?" (Five Forces.)
- "How can BEKO compete in a mature industry?" (Cost, new segments, differentiation, innovation.)
- "Evaluate the Beko–Whirlpool deal as an international growth strategy."

**9. Summary.** **BEKO = a value-for-money appliance leader growing by scale and acquisition (Beko Europe) in a mature, price-competitive industry; the key issues are integration and restructuring.**

## 4. MyStyleBags — S4 (Leopoldo Orlando)
> *From the lecture, plus public company information.*

**1. Overview.**
- A Milan company making **bags, duffel bags and travel accessories**, **founded in 2008 by Lorenza Bellora**. `[REC]`
- The founder brings over 30 years' experience from her family's business, **Bellora 1883**, which she ran with her brother Giuseppe. Growth accelerated after **Stefano Donadel Campbell** joined. `[REC]`
- About **22–23 stores**, mostly in Italy, plus a growing international presence (Paris, London, Madrid). `[REC]`

**2. Business model.**
- **Three founding pillars:** **tradition** (artisan heritage), **Made in Italy** and **customisation**. `[REC]`
- **Personalisation:** customers choose materials and colours and add initials, so a standard product becomes a personal one. `[PN]`
- **Channels:**
  - mostly **directly operated mono-brand stores**;
  - temporary formats such as a **pop-up at Le Bon Marché** in Paris;
  - online marketplaces such as Wolf & Badger. `[REC]`
- **A wide product range** across personal use, travel, gifts and **corporate/hospitality** orders. `[PN]`
- **Co-branding:** for example, a collaboration with Portofino Dry Gin. `[REC]`

**3. Positioning.**
- **Premium yet accessible:** quality, Italian craft and personalisation at prices below luxury houses. `[PN]`
- **Brand mood:** "old money", La Dolce Vita, slow life, **no visible logo**. This is **understated, quiet luxury**, the opposite of logo-driven fashion. `[REC]`
- **Competitive set discussed in class:** RIMOWA (premium travel), Longchamp and Coccinelle (accessible premium), MC2 Saint Barth (resort lifestyle). `[PN]`

**4. Main challenges.**
- **A crowded accessories market** with low entry barriers for generic bags. Personalisation must stay distinctive.
- **International growth:**
  - Europe is the strongest market, led by Italy, Spain, Switzerland and Germany;
  - the company is pushing harder into **France and the US**. `[REC]`
- **Scaling customisation:** personalised production must stay fast and cost-efficient as volumes grow. `[REC]`

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Customisation capability** (processes and craftsmanship).
- **Family know-how** in leather goods and travel products since 1883.
- A **store network** that delivers the personalisation experience in person.
- A **corporate gifting / B2B** relationship base.

**6. Frameworks.**
- **S4 segmentation:**
  - by **usage occasion** (personal, travel, gifts, corporate/hospitality);
  - by gender, geography and income/price. `[PN]`
- **S4 strategic groups:** MyStyleBags sits between accessible-premium fashion brands and premium travel brands. Personalisation is its **mobility barrier**.
- **S7 differentiation:**
  - personalisation is **tangible** (product features) and **intangible** (identity, emotional value);
  - it raises **willingness to pay** and lowers **price sensitivity**. `[PN]`
- **S12 internationalisation:**
  - Paris, London and Madrid are culturally close (CAGE);
  - the US push faces greater distance.

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Personalisation at the core | Differentiation, loyalty and gifting demand; more complex operations |
| No-logo, understated style | Fits the quiet-luxury trend; needs strong store experience to build awareness |
| Pop-ups and marketplaces abroad | Low-risk market testing (**opportunistic / phased-in entry**) |
| Corporate/hospitality segment | B2B volume and visibility; different buying criteria from B2C |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Segment MyStyleBags' market and identify its most attractive segments." (Use the segmentation matrix and KSFs.)
- "Draw a strategic group map for premium bags and travel goods; where is MyStyleBags positioned?"
- "How does personalisation create competitive advantage? Is it sustainable?" (VRIO.)

**9. Summary.** **MyStyleBags = a Milan family-heritage brand (2008) built on tradition, Made in Italy and personalisation; accessible, no-logo premium; segments by usage, including corporate gifts.**

## 5. Chantecler — S7 (Vittoria Carandini)
> *From the lecture, plus public company information.*

**1. Overview.**
- A fine-jewellery house from **Capri**.
- **Founders:** **Pietro Capuano**, a Neapolitan jeweller nicknamed "Chantecler", and **Salvatore Aprea**, a young Capri local who left his law studies for jewellery. `[REC]`
- The brand dates itself to **1947**; the boutique on Capri's via Vittorio Emanuele opened around **1950**. `[REC]`
- It is still family-led by the **Aprea** family.
- It is an "atypical" luxury brand because it **comes from Capri, not Rome, Milan, Paris or New York**. `[REC]`

**2. Business model.**
- Designs and sells fine jewellery: **High Jewelry → Prêt-à-porter / Icons → silver "Et Voilà"** for everyday wear. `[PN]`
- Production in **Valenza**, Italy's jewellery district. Artisan techniques (enamel, engraving, hand-shaped coral and turquoise) are combined with **3D modelling and micro-casting**. `[PN]`
- **Channels:** Chantecler boutiques plus selected international retailers. **International expansion began in 2003.** `[REC]`
- **Brand experience beyond the product:**
  - the ***Capritude*** magazine, events and branded gifts `[PN]`;
  - the **Casa Chantecler** museum project, launched for the Campanella's 80th anniversary in 2024. `[REC]`

**3. Positioning.**
- Luxury as **identity, independence and joy** (the spirit of Capri and La Dolce Vita), not conspicuous status. Its tagline is "*Tu sei un'isola*". `[PN]`
- **Signature product: the Campanella.**
  - Its story goes back to **1944**, when Capuano gave a real bell to US President Franklin D. Roosevelt as a **symbol of peace and friendship**. `[REC]`
  - In class it was presented as the Bell of Saint Michael, a lucky charm. `[PN]`
- **Celebrity heritage:**
  - clients have included Ingrid Bergman, Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis, Audrey Hepburn and Mona von Bismarck;
  - each has inspired a **limited-edition Campanella**. `[REC]`

**4. Main challenges.**
- **Renewing heritage without copying it:** the Campanella must stay recognisable but feel contemporary. `[PN]`
- **A broader range with a coherent identity:** Et Voilà must not dilute high jewellery. `[PN]`
- **International growth for a small maison** competing against Bulgari, Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels.
- **Reaching younger collectors:** Italians remain the largest buyers, but a **younger, international** collector base is emerging. `[REC]`

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Heritage and story:** the founders, the 1944 bell, Capri, its celebrity clients.
- **An iconic, recognisable design** (the Campanella).
- **Valenza craftsmanship** combined with modern techniques.
- An **archive** (letters and documents shown at Vicenzaoro 2024).
- Family ownership, which gives long-term focus.

**6. Frameworks.**
- **S7 differentiation:**
  - **intangible** (story, symbol, identity);
  - **signals** (jewellery's value is hard to judge before purchase, so brand, story and celebrity heritage matter);
  - drivers of uniqueness: input quality, artisan skills, location (Capri/Valenza), control of production.
- **S7 generic strategy:** **differentiation focus**.
- **S5 VRIO:** heritage and the Campanella story are **valuable, rare, hard to imitate** (path-dependent and historically unique) and **organised**: the magazine, boutiques and archive exploit them. The result is a **sustained advantage**.
- **S14 BMC:** a premium value proposition, an experience-driven channel and customer relationships.

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Keeping the Campanella shape, renewing materials and themes | Recognition plus collectability; risk of over-using the icon |
| Et Voilà silver line | Reaches younger clients and everyday use; must protect premium perception |
| Limited editions named after icons (e.g., Ingrid Bergman, Jackie Kennedy Onassis) | Turns heritage into product news and scarcity |
| Casa Chantecler museum, *Capritude*, events | Makes heritage visible; deepens the brand experience beyond purchase |
| International expansion from 2003 | Growth; must keep Capri authenticity abroad |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "How does Chantecler create differentiation advantage? Distinguish tangible from intangible elements and explain the role of signals."
- "Apply VRIO to Chantecler's heritage and to the Campanella design."
- "How can a heritage brand renew itself without losing its identity?"

**9. Summary.** **Chantecler = a Capri maison (1947) whose Campanella (dating to a 1944 peace bell) and Dolce Vita heritage drive intangible differentiation; Valenza craftsmanship, storytelling (*Capritude*, Casa Chantecler) and limited editions keep the icon fresh.**

## 6. EssilorLuxottica — S11 (Andrea Mecacci)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- The world's largest eyewear group, formed in **2018** by merging **Essilor** (French lenses) and **Luxottica** (Italian frames and retail).
- **Brands:** Ray-Ban, Oakley and luxury licences.
- **Retail:** Sunglass Hut, LensCrafters, GrandVision.
- **FY2025:** revenue up **11.2%** at constant exchange rates (+18.4% in Q4), adjusted operating margin **16.0%**.

**2. Business model.**
- **Fully vertically integrated:** lens R&D → frame design and manufacturing → wholesale → own retail.
- It increasingly presents itself as moving **"from an optical company into a medtech and big-data group"**: myopia-control lenses (Stellest), hearing-aid glasses (Nuance Audio) and AI health platforms.

**3. Positioning.**
- A dominant leader across the value chain.
- **The smart-glasses leader** through its **partnership with Meta**, which started with Ray-Ban Stories in **2021**.
- **Over 7 million AI glasses sold in 2025** (Ray-Ban Meta, Oakley Meta), more than triple 2024.
- Prices range from $299 (original Stories) to **$799** (Meta Ray-Ban Display).

**4. Main challenges.**
- Smart glasses are **less profitable** than traditional products and dilute margins.
- **Dependence on Meta** for technology and the platform.
- Coming competition from big tech (Google, Apple, Samsung, Xiaomi) in a category where the **standard is not yet set**.
- Tariffs, and privacy concerns around camera glasses.

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Iconic brands** (Ray-Ban, Oakley).
- **Global manufacturing and retail reach.**
- **Lens technology.**
- **Complementary assets** that tech firms lack: design, fit, optical prescriptions, distribution.

**6. Frameworks (S11).**
- **Complementary resources and appropriability** (Teece): Meta brings the technology, EssilorLuxottica the brands, manufacturing and channels. Each side captures value through what it controls.
- **Strategic alliance** rather than internal commercialisation, which shares risk and reduces market uncertainty.
- **First mover and standards:** early leadership in smart glasses can build an installed base and set the de facto standard.
- **S5:** VRIO of brands and retail.
- **S14–15:** business model innovation (new revenue from tech products and health services).

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Alliance with Meta | Fast access to AI and AR technology; shared risk; but dependence on a powerful partner |
| Expanding the smart-glasses range (Oakley Meta, Display) | Market leadership and growth; lower short-term margins |
| Medtech pivot (myopia, hearing) | New, defensible growth areas that use its lens know-how |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Using EssilorLuxottica and Meta, explain the role of complementary resources in profiting from innovation."
- "Is EssilorLuxottica a first mover in smart glasses? What determines whether it will capture the value?"
- "Assess the risks of the Meta alliance."

**9. Summary.** **EssilorLuxottica = a vertically integrated eyewear giant using its brands and channels as complementary assets in an alliance with Meta to lead smart glasses (over 7m units in 2025).**

## 7. L'Oréal — S13 (Stefania Bertolino)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- The **world's No. 1 beauty company**.
- **2025 sales: €44.05bn** (+4.0% like-for-like), operating margin **20.2%**.
- About **40 international brands**.
- E-commerce now **over 30% of sales**.

**2. Business model.**
- **Four divisions**, each with its own consumer universe and channels:
  - **Consumer Products** (37%: L'Oréal Paris, Garnier, Maybelline);
  - **Luxe** (35%: Lancôme, YSL Beauty, Armani);
  - **Dermatological Beauty** (16%: La Roche-Posay, CeraVe);
  - **Professional Products** (12%: Kérastase).
- **Production close to markets.**
- Heavy R&I and marketing.
- 2025 acquisitions include **Kering Beauté** (with Creed) and a larger stake in **Galderma**.

**3. Positioning.**
- **"Beauty for each"**: global brands adapted to local skin, hair, climate and culture.
- L'Oréal has described its approach as **universalisation**: globalisation that respects differences.

**4. Main challenges.**
- Different beauty needs across regions.
- Slower luxury demand in China.
- Competition from local and indie brands.
- Fast-moving social-media trends.
- Sustainability: packaging and refills.

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- The brand portfolio.
- **Global R&I hubs** that develop region-specific formulas.
- Retailer partnerships (Sephora, Douglas, mass retail).
- **"One L'Oréal"** scale combined with local agility.
- Beauty tech and AI (diagnostics, recommendations).

**6. Frameworks (S12–13).**
- **A global industry with multidomestic demand**, met with the **combined approach**: global brands and science, local adaptation of products and marketing.
- **"Think global, act local"**, which leans toward the **transnational** model (knowledge flows between regional hubs).
- **CAGE:** cultural distance is high in beauty.
- **Entry modes:** **acquisitions** of local brands to enter or strengthen markets.
- **Link to S16:** refills and packaging intensity (from an earlier L'Oréal guest talk).

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Multi-division portfolio | Covers every price point and channel; reduces risk |
| Local R&I and production | Responsiveness to local needs; higher complexity |
| Acquisitions (Kering Beauté, Galderma stake) | Faster entry into luxury fragrance and aesthetics; integration risk |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Is beauty a global or multidomestic industry? How does L'Oréal manage the tension?"
- "Explain L'Oréal's balance between standardisation and customisation, with examples."

**9. Summary.** **L'Oréal = the global No. 1 in beauty; global brands and science, adapted locally ("universalisation"), with four divisions and growth through acquisitions.**

## 8. Amazon Web Services — S14 (Stefano Saccà)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- Amazon's cloud division, launched in **2006**.
- **2025 revenue: about $128.7bn**, operating income about **$45.6bn**.
- That is about **18% of Amazon's sales but about 57% of its operating profit**.

**2. Business model.**
- **Infrastructure-as-a-service** (computing, storage, databases, AI) sold **pay-as-you-go**.
- Customers turn IT capital spending into variable cost.
- Revenue comes from usage fees plus reserved and committed contracts. **Customer commitments (backlog): about $244bn** at the end of 2025.

**3. Positioning.**
- **The market leader** (about **28–32% share** in Q4 2025), ahead of **Microsoft Azure (about 21–22%)** and **Google Cloud (about 11–14%)**.
- AWS grows more slowly in percentage terms (+20% in 2025; +24% in Q4) than its rivals, but adds the most absolute revenue.

**4. Main challenges.**
- **The AI race:** rivals gain share with AI offerings.
- **Huge capital needs:** Amazon spent about **$132bn** in 2025 and guided about **$200bn** for 2026, mostly for AWS.
- Energy and data-centre capacity.
- Regulation and data sovereignty.

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Scale** (global data centres).
- **First-mover experience** ("no compression algorithm for experience").
- **Custom chips** (Graviton, Trainium).
- A broad service ecosystem and partner network.
- Amazon's culture: customer obsession, working backwards, two-pizza teams, one-way vs. two-way doors (from an earlier AWS guest talk).

**6. Frameworks.**
- **S14:** BMC and revenue model (**usage fee / pay-as-you-go**).
- **S6:** economies of **scale and learning**.
- **S11:** first-mover advantage, **standards** and switching costs.
- **S5:** VRIO.
- **S15:** a **market explorer** pattern (new technology for proven demand).

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Pay-as-you-go pricing | Lowers barriers for start-ups; builds a massive installed base |
| Massive AI capex | Defends leadership; big financial risk if demand slows |
| Multi-model AI (Bedrock) and partnerships | Customer choice; positions AWS as a neutral platform |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Draw the BMC of AWS and identify its revenue model."
- "Why has AWS sustained leadership in cloud? Is the advantage sustainable as Azure and Google grow faster?"

**9. Summary.** **AWS = the pay-as-you-go cloud leader (about 30% share, about $129bn revenue in 2025), built on scale, experience and customer obsession, now defending leadership in the AI race.**

## 9. IVECO — S16 (Carla De Luca)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- A Turin-based maker of **commercial vehicles**:
  - Daily vans, Eurocargo and S-Way trucks;
  - **IVECO BUS**;
  - **FPT Industrial** powertrains.
- Spun off from **CNH Industrial in 2022**.
- **In 2025 the group split up:**
  - its **defence business** (IDV, Astra) went to **Leonardo** (€1.7bn agreed);
  - **Tata Motors** launched a €3.8bn offer for the rest.
- **Tata's takeover completed in November 2026**, so IVECO is now part of Tata Motors.

**2. Business model.** It designs and manufactures trucks, buses and engines, sold through dealers to fleets and public transport operators, plus after-sales services and parts.

**3. Positioning.**
- **A "multi-energy" / technology-neutral approach:** the right powertrain for each mission.
  - **HVO and biomethane / natural gas** (IVECO is the **European leader in natural-gas commercial vehicles**: Stralis NP in 2016, today's S-Way CNG/LNG).
  - **Battery-electric** (eDaily, S-eWay).
  - **Hydrogen fuel cell** (partnership with **Hyundai**).
- **Bio-methane can cut CO₂ by up to 95%** compared with diesel.

**4. Main challenges.**
- **EU CO₂ targets** for heavy-duty vehicles.
- High cost and limited charging infrastructure for electric trucks.
- Weak demand cycles (revenue fell 10% in Q1 2025).
- Competing against larger rivals (Daimler, Volvo, Traton).
- **Integration into Tata.**

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- Natural-gas engine leadership (FPT).
- European manufacturing and dealer network.
- Partnerships (Hyundai for electric/hydrogen, Ford Otosan for cabins, Stellantis for electric vans).
- Bus contracts (for example, up to 4,000 buses for Île-de-France Mobilités).

**6. Frameworks (S16).**
- **Approaches to sustainability:** between **adaptive** (turning regulation into product opportunities, as Toyota did) and **proactive**.
- **Competitive sustainability matrix:**
  - **sustainable cost leadership** (natural-gas trucks with diesel-like total cost of ownership);
  - **sustainable branding** (green transport offers).
- **Selective sustainability:** for example, battery supply chains.
- **S11:** managing technological uncertainty by keeping options open (multi-energy as **real options**).
- **S12 / S18:** cross-border M&A (Tata), divesting non-core defence (**downscoping**).

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Multi-energy rather than a single bet | Flexibility under technological uncertainty; higher R&D spread across technologies |
| Leading in biomethane | A ready-now decarbonisation solution; risk if regulation favours zero-tailpipe vehicles |
| Sale of defence + Tata takeover | Focus on the core; access to Tata's scale in India and Asia; integration challenges |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Classify IVECO's approach to sustainability and the competitive sustainability strategy it follows."
- "Is a multi-energy strategy wise under technological uncertainty?" (Link to S11.)
- "Assess the Tata acquisition from IVECO's perspective."

**9. Summary.** **IVECO = a European truck and bus maker decarbonising through a multi-energy strategy (biomethane leader, electric, hydrogen), now part of Tata Motors since November 2026.**

## 10. Cassina — S19 (Andrea Bocchiola)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- An Italian high-end design furniture company **founded in 1927** in Meda (Brianza).
- Part of the **Poltrona Frau Group** (with Poltrona Frau and Cappellini) from the mid-2000s.
- Acquired by the US group **Haworth in 2014**; the group was renamed **Haworth Lifestyle** in 2025 and has since added brands such as **Zanotta**.

**2. Business model.**
- Contemporary collections with leading designers (Patricia Urquiola is art director).
- The **"I Maestri" collection** (launched **1973**): **authorised reissues** of 20th-century masterpieces based on careful **philological research**.
- In **1964** Cassina obtained the worldwide rights to produce the furniture of **Le Corbusier, Pierre Jeanneret and Charlotte Perriand** (LC collection).
- **Channels:** mono-brand stores (13 openings in 2023 alone), dealers, and a contract division (Cassina Custom Interiors).

**3. Positioning.** **Luxury, authenticity and design culture**: the "original" against the copy.

**4. Main challenges.**
- **Replicas and knock-offs** of iconic designs, sold at a fraction of the price.
- Licences that depend on designers' estates.
- International expansion.
- Contested authorship: Cassina lost a 2017 dispute with Molteni&C over a Gio Ponti chair.

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Exclusive licences** (Fondation Le Corbusier and the heirs).
- Archives and philological know-how.
- Craftsmanship.
- Heritage brand.
- Membership of the Haworth group (scale, North American contract market).

**6. Frameworks (S19).**
- **Defensive strategies:**
  - **raising structural barriers** through IP, exclusive licences and lawsuits against copies;
  - **fortify and defend** the core (I Maestri);
  - **signalling** (authenticity marks, renaming the "Collection Le Corbusier®", restoring original names);
  - **cover all bases** (contemporary, outdoor and contract lines).
- **Legal defence examples:**
  - *Peek & Cloppenburg v Cassina* (European Court of Justice, 2008);
  - Italian court rulings against replica sellers;
  - a **Court of Rome** ruling against RAI's use of LC2 copies.
- **S5:** VRIO (exclusive licences and heritage are rare and legally protected).
- **S7:** intangible differentiation.

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Exclusive licences + litigation | Protects margins and authenticity; legal costs; mixed outcomes in court |
| Own stores worldwide | Control of experience and brand; high fixed costs |
| Joining Haworth | Access to global contract channels; must protect brand identity inside a group |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Which defensive strategies does Cassina use against replica producers?"
- "Is Cassina's competitive advantage sustainable? Apply VRIO to its licences and heritage."

**9. Summary.** **Cassina = authentic Italian design since 1927; defends the "original" (I Maestri, Le Corbusier licence) through IP, litigation and signalling; part of Haworth Lifestyle.**

## 11. McKinsey & Company — S22 (Alberto Uccelli)
> No lecture material is available. Researched profile.

**1. Overview.**
- A global management consulting firm **founded in 1926 in Chicago** by **James O. McKinsey**.
- **Marvin Bower** shaped its professional partnership model.
- One of the "MBB" strategy firms, with BCG and Bain.

**2. Business model.**
- A **partnership** selling expertise through **project fees**.
- **Value proposition:** insight, objectivity and implementation support for top management.
- **Key resources:** people (highly selective hiring), proprietary knowledge, reputation, alumni network.

**3. Positioning.** Premium "trusted adviser" to CEOs and governments; strategy, operations, digital and AI transformation.

**4. Main challenges.**
- **Reputation risk:** in 2021 it paid about $573m to settle US opioid-related claims.
- Competition from Big Four advisory firms and boutiques.
- Clients building internal strategy teams.
- **AI changing the analyst pyramid.**

**5. Resources and capabilities.**
- **Structured problem solving:** SCQ, issue trees, **MECE**, hypothesis-driven work, 80/20.
- **Communication:** the **Pyramid Principle** (Barbara Minto, a former McKinsey consultant).
- Frameworks such as the **7S** and **Three Horizons**.

**6. Frameworks.**
- **S22:** consulting toolkit (see the S22 chapter).
- **S14:** BMC of a professional-services firm.
- **S5:** VRIO (reputation and networks are valuable, rare and hard to imitate).
- **S18:** consultants as an outside force against inertia.

**7. Decisions and implications.**
| Decision | Implication |
|---|---|
| Up-or-out talent model | High quality and a strong alumni network; high turnover |
| Expanding into implementation and digital | Larger, longer projects; competes with IT firms |
| Investing in AI tools | Productivity; pressure on the junior pyramid |

**8. Exam questions.**
- "Describe the structured problem-solving approach of strategy consultants."
- "What makes a top consulting firm's advantage sustainable?" (VRIO.)

**9. Summary.** **McKinsey = the archetypal strategy firm (since 1926); its edge is people, reputation and structured problem solving (MECE, issue trees, pyramid).**
