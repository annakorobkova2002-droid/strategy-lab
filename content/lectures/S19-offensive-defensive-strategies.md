# S19 — Offensive vs. Defensive Strategies

## 1. Learning objectives
- Define offensive and defensive strategies and their objectives.
- Describe the spectrum of offensive strategies, from direct struggle to avoidance, with examples.
- Explain the three defensive mechanisms.
- Distinguish pre-entry from post-entry defences, and know how to respond when attacked.
- Analyse the pickup "truck wars" (RAM, GM, Ford).

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Offensive strategy** | Improving one's competitive position by **taking market share from competitors** through direct or indirect attacks. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Defensive strategy** | Lowering the **probability and impact** of attacks, mainly by making attacks **unattractive** to rivals and potential entrants. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Flank attack** | Attacking a competitor's weak points or blind spots. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Bypass (leapfrog) attack** | Overtaking competitors by moving to new technologies or markets instead of fighting head-on. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Fighting brand** | A separate brand created to fight a specific challenger, protecting the main brand's reputation. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Pivot & hammer** | Combining defence (pivot: hold position and customers) with concentrated attack (hammer). `[PN][SD]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Who attacks, who defends? `[SD]`
- **Usually:** new entrants attack (they need customers); incumbents defend (they protect position).
- **But:** incumbents can attack too, for example to exploit a **shakeout** and knock out weaker players. New entrants can also defend.

### 3.2 Offensive strategies `[PN][SD]`
**Objectives:**
- destabilise the leader;
- acquire market share;
- boost sales (sometimes temporarily, to hit targets);
- leapfrog competitors.

Offensives can be retaliatory and are carefully calculated.

**The spectrum:** from **direct confrontation** to **elusion** (avoiding direct contact). `[PN][SD]`
| # | Strategy | Explanation | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Direct struggle (frontal)** | Head-on attack with similar products, price, quality, promotion and distribution. Targets the rival's **strengths**; high risk unless there is a clear advantage; reveals your intentions | BMW vs. Mercedes `[PN]` |
| 2 | **Flank attack** | Hit weak points or blind spots; the path of least resistance; less risky | AMD vs. Intel in mid-range processors `[PN]`; Samsung vs. Apple in AI and foldables `[SD]` |
| 3 | **Strategic encirclement** | Frontal plus flank attacks on many fronts or brands at once; needs **superior resources**; spreads the defender thin | Amazon vs. traditional retailers `[PN]` |
| 4 | **Guerrilla attack** | Small, repeated hit-and-run attacks (for example, below-cost promotions) that destabilise; too costly to sustain for long | Shein flash sales and social marketing `[PN]`; Ferrero–Barilla skirmishes `[SD]` |
| 5 | **Bypass attack (leapfrog)** | Overtake through new technology, or avoid the incumbent via a significant niche | Skype vs. traditional telecoms `[PN]`; Brondi phones for the elderly `[SD]` |
| 6 | **Seek undefended markets** | Enter neglected segments or diversify (Blue Ocean logic) | Alpro in plant-based milk `[PN]` |
| 7 | **Underdog** | Offer an attractive alternative to established brands | Under Armour vs. Nike and Adidas `[PN]` |
| 8 | **Predatory** | Accept lower profits or price below cost to keep entrants out or force rivals to exit | Ryanair's aggressive pricing `[PN]`; Amazon matching offers `[SD]` |
| 9 | **Judo** | Use the rival's size and rigidity against it; attack where it is uncomfortable defending | Netflix vs. Blockbuster `[PN]` |
| 10 | **Pivot & hammer** | Defend the core while concentrating the attack | Slack `[PN]` |

> Your notes add a caution: aggressive low prices (Ryanair) are not proof of *unlawful* predatory pricing.

### 3.3 Defensive strategies `[PN][SD]`
**Objectives:**
- protect market share, profitability, reputation, customer confidence and market leadership;
- raise entry barriers (even when no entrant is visible);
- long-term contracts ⚠ (your notes say "secure" them; the 2024–25 notes say "avoid" them, because a damaged firm may be unable to honour them; audit C2).

A war is costly for both sides, so it is better to discourage the attack.

**Rivals locked in mutual defence and response** `[SD]`: Apple–Samsung, Aldi–Lidl, Coca-Cola–Pepsi. Iliad's aggressive pricing triggered rival offers, especially for students.

### 3.4 Three defensive mechanisms `[PN][SD]`
1. **Raise structural barriers:**
   - fill product or market gaps;
   - raise buyer **switching costs** (Nespresso machines that only take Nespresso capsules `[SD]`);
   - block channel access;
   - continuous improvement;
   - raise capital requirements;
   - lobby for barrier-raising policies;
   - form coalitions (taxi lobbies vs. Uber; airline alliances `[SD]`).
2. **Raise the perceived threat of counterattack:** signal barriers, establish blocking positions, encourage "good" competitors, form coalitions.
3. **Reduce the benefits of an attack:** lower the challenger's expected profits; manage competitors' assumptions.

**Successful deterrence requires** understanding existing barriers, identifying the likely challengers, and anticipating their moves.

### 3.5 Pre-entry vs. post-entry strategies `[PN][SD]`
**Pre-entry** (before competitors enter): prevent or discourage entry.
| Strategy | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Signalling** | Show strength and the ability to retaliate (for example, the ability to cut prices sharply) | Pfizer announcing its R&D pipeline `[PN]` |
| **Fortify and defend** | Strengthen barriers around the core revenue source | Apple's ecosystem and switching costs `[PN]`; Coca-Cola guarding its classic line `[SD]` |
| **Cover all bases** | Occupy all segments so no gap is left | P&G's brand portfolio `[PN]`; Samsung following Xiaomi into washing machines `[SD]` |
| **Continuous improvement** | Keep raising the bar so entry is too costly | Toyota's kaizen `[PN]`; yearly iPhones, AI features `[SD]` |
| **Capacity extension** | Expand capacity or lock up channels | Shell investing in renewables `[PN]`; top brands occupying all high-end retail space `[SD]` |

**Post-entry** (after competitors enter): stop the entrant from becoming established.
| Strategy | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Early defence** | React before the entrant grows | Microsoft vs. Google Docs `[PN]` |
| **Fighting products** | A product within your own range aimed at the challenger | Volkswagen affordable models `[PN]`; Vodafone offers for Iliad switchers `[SD]` |
| **Fighting brands** | A separate brand aimed at the challenger | Coca-Cola's Smartwater `[PN]`; **Kena (TIM)** and **ho. (Vodafone)** vs. Iliad; **Transavia** (Air France–KLM) vs. Ryanair `[SD]` |

### 3.6 If attacked `[PN][SD]`
1. Respond **as early as possible**.
2. Address the **reasons** for the attack.
3. Don't just stop the challenger; **deflect** it.
4. Treat the response as an **opportunity to gain position**.

Example: Barilla's Crema Pan di Stelle attacked Nutella. Ferrero counterattacked with biscuits but still lost share. `[SD]`

## 4. Optional cases VII–VIII — RAM, GM and Ford: the truck wars `[PN]` + `[REC]`
**Context:** full-size pickups are among the most profitable vehicles in the US. Ford (F-Series), GM (Chevrolet Silverado) and RAM (Stellantis) compete fiercely.

**RAM's offensive** `[PN]`:
- high-quality interiors (differentiation);
- a large 12-inch touchscreen (technological differentiation);
- positioning pickups as **family vehicles** (segmentation, flank attack).

According to the case, RAM **tripled its sales** and overtook Chevrolet to become the #2 full-size truck brand at the time.

**The HEMI episode** `[PN]`:
- RAM replaced its iconic **HEMI V8** with a twin-turbo engine;
- this caused customer dissatisfaction, falling sales and weaker loyalty;
- RAM then **reintroduced the HEMI** while planning electric trucks.

**Interpretation** `[PN]`:
- the HEMI comeback is a **defensive move** to protect loyalty;
- electric trucks are an adaptation to technological change;
- keeping both balances innovation with existing preferences.

**GM and Ford** `[REC]`: as incumbents, they used **continuous improvement** and **cover all bases** (trims, powertrains, including Ford's F-150 Lightning EV) to defend leadership.

**Lesson:** innovation must not destroy the differentiation sources that existing customers value.

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Offensive: gain share | Defensive: protect share, deter attacks |
| Direct struggle: attacks strengths | Flank attack: attacks weaknesses |
| Guerrilla: small, repeated, temporary | Encirclement: massive, many fronts |
| Fighting product: same brand | Fighting brand: separate brand |
| Pre-entry: prevent entry | Post-entry: block establishment |
| Bypass: new technology, avoids the fight | Judo: uses the rival's own weight |

## 7. Exam traps
- **Direct struggle targets strengths; flank attack targets weaknesses.**
- **A fighting brand ≠ a fighting product.** Kena and ho. are *brands*.
- **Signalling, fortifying, covering bases, continuous improvement and capacity extension = pre-entry. Early defence, fighting products and fighting brands = post-entry.**
- Seeking undefended markets is the offensive cousin of Blue Ocean.
- Remember the S8 contradiction (C1): use these S19 definitions.

## 9. Memorise this
- **Offensive = take share; defensive = make attacks unattractive.**
- **Offensive spectrum:** direct · flank · encirclement · guerrilla · bypass · undefended markets · underdog · predatory · judo · pivot & hammer.
- **Defence mechanisms:** structural barriers · threat of retaliation · lower benefits of attack.
- **Pre-entry:** signalling · fortify · cover all bases · continuous improvement · capacity extension.
- **Post-entry:** early defence · fighting products · fighting brands.
- **If attacked:** early · target the reasons · deflect · gain position.
- **RAM:** interiors + touchscreen + family positioning → #2; HEMI removal backfired → reintroduced.
