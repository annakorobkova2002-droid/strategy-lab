# S9–S10 — Blue Ocean Strategy

## 1. Learning objectives
- Distinguish red-ocean from blue-ocean strategy.
- Explain value innovation and how it breaks the value–cost trade-off.
- Follow the five-step Blue Ocean process: the PMS map, AS-IS canvas, buyer utility map, three tiers of noncustomers, four actions/ERRC, six paths, TO-BE canvas, litmus test.
- Apply the tools to Cirque du Soleil, Nintendo Wii, Yellow Tail and Tesla.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Red ocean** | Existing industries with defined, accepted boundaries and clear rules. Firms fight for share; as competitors multiply, profits shrink. `[SD]` |
| **Blue ocean** | Uncontested market space that creates **new demand**, making competition irrelevant. `[SD][PN]` |
| **Value innovation** | The simultaneous pursuit of **differentiation and low cost**, creating a leap in value for buyers and for the company. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Strategy canvas** | A chart of an industry's competing factors (x-axis) and the offering level buyers receive on each (y-axis). `[PN][SD]` |
| **Value curve** | The graphic depiction of a company's relative performance across the industry's competing factors. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Noncustomers** | People outside the industry's current customer base: potential new demand. `[PN]` |
| **ERRC grid** | Eliminate–Reduce–Raise–Create: the matrix that puts the four actions into practice. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Litmus test** | A good blue ocean value curve shows **focus, divergence and a compelling tagline**. `[PN][SD]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Red vs. blue oceans `[SD]`
| Red ocean | Blue ocean |
|---|---|
| Compete in existing market space | Create uncontested market space |
| Beat the competition | Make the competition irrelevant |
| Exploit existing demand | Create and capture new demand |
| Make the value–cost trade-off | Break the value–cost trade-off |
| Align the whole system with *either* differentiation *or* low cost | Align the whole system with differentiation *and* low cost |

**Key claims** `[SD]`:
- There are **two ways** to create a blue ocean:
  - a completely new industry (eBay, online auctions);
  - more often, **reshaping boundaries inside a red ocean** (Cirque du Soleil, between circus and theatre).
- Blue oceans are **not mainly about technology**: they link existing technology to what buyers value.
- They are often created by **incumbents** inside their core business.
- They **don't last forever**: imitators eventually arrive.

### 3.2 Value innovation `[PN][SD]`
- **Cost savings:** *eliminate* and *reduce* factors the industry competes on.
- **Buyer value:** *raise* and *create* elements the industry has never offered.

So the firm doesn't need to offer more of everything. It drops what buyers don't value and invests in what they do.

Link to S7: this is the "combined" strategy that Porter warned could leave a firm stuck in the middle. Blue Ocean argues it can work.

**Marvel example** `[SD]`:
- the connected "Infinity Saga" reused cast and visual-effects learning across films (lower average cost);
- the interlinked stories built loyalty and community (more value).

### 3.3 Cirque du Soleil `[PN][SD]`
The circus industry was declining: animal-welfare concerns, costly star performers, children's audiences, the same show every time.

| Action | Cirque du Soleil |
|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | Animal shows, star performers, multiple show arenas |
| **Reduce** | Fun & humour, thrill & danger (kept but made more refined) |
| **Raise** | Unique venue (the tent), artistic quality |
| **Create** | Theme/storyline, refined environment, multiple productions, artistic music and dance |

**Result:** lower costs (no animals or stars) plus a theatre-like experience. The new audience was adults and corporate clients, who paid premium prices.

### 3.4 The five-step process `[PN][SD]` (Blue Ocean Shift)
| Step | Objective | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Get started** | Choose the offering with the greatest potential | **Pioneer–Migrator–Settler map** |
| 2. **Understand the current state of play** | See how the industry competes | **AS-IS strategy canvas** |
| 3. **Explore what could be** | Find pain points and new demand | **Buyer utility map**, **three tiers of noncustomers** |
| 4. **Define how to get there** | Redesign the value proposition | **Four actions framework / ERRC**, **six paths**, **TO-BE canvas** |
| 5. **Make the blue ocean move** | Select and test the strategic move | **Litmus test** (and business-model refinement) |

**Step 1, Pioneer–Migrator–Settler** `[SD]`:
- **Pioneers** offer unprecedented value.
- **Migrators** offer value better than most.
- **Settlers** are "me-too" offerings.

A portfolio dominated by settlers is strategically vulnerable. Example: Apple's portfolio of pioneer products.

### 3.5 AS-IS strategy canvas `[PN][SD]`
**How to build it:**
1. Define the industry and its main competitors.
2. List the competing factors.
3. Score each competitor's offering level.
4. Draw the value curves.
5. Interpret them.

**What it shows:**
- Similar curves mean firms are competing head-on in a red ocean.
- It reorients attention from competitors to **alternatives**, and from customers to **noncustomers**.
- It creates a shared baseline for change.

The AS-IS canvas **describes** the present; it does not prescribe the future.

### 3.6 Buyer utility map `[PN][SD]`
A 6 × 6 matrix crossing:
- **Six stages of the buyer experience cycle:** purchase → delivery → use → supplements → maintenance → disposal.
- **Six utility levers:** customer productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk reduction, fun & image, environmental friendliness.

**Purpose:** find pain points and unlocked utility from the *demand side*. The key question: does the new idea remove the biggest blocks to utility that keep noncustomers out?

### 3.7 Three tiers of noncustomers `[PN][SD]`
| Tier | Who | Barrier | Example (Nintendo Wii) `[SD]` |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st: "soon-to-be"** | On the edge of the market, buying minimally, ready to leave | Dissatisfaction | Marginal gamers (young adults, students) |
| **2nd: "refusing"** | Know the market but consciously reject it | Unaffordable, unappealing or unsuitable offers | Tried and refused (sports fans, readers) |
| **3rd: "unexplored"** | In distant markets; never considered the offering | Unawareness or irrelevance | The elderly, very young children |

**Key principle:** look for **commonalities among noncustomers**, rather than refining differences between existing customers.

### 3.8 Four actions framework and ERRC grid `[PN][SD]`
| Action | Question |
|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | Which factors the industry takes for granted should be eliminated? |
| **Reduce** | Which factors should be reduced well **below** the industry standard? |
| **Raise** | Which factors should be raised well **above** the industry standard? |
| **Create** | Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? |

The four actions framework gives the questions; the **ERRC grid** records the answers as concrete decisions.
- Eliminate ≠ reduce: *eliminate* removes a factor entirely; *reduce* keeps it with less investment.
- Raise ≠ create: *raise* improves an existing factor; *create* adds a new one.

### 3.9 Six paths framework: reconstructing market boundaries `[PN]`
| Path | Look across… | Example (from your notes) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Alternative industries** | Different industries satisfying the same need | Cinema vs. home entertainment |
| 2. **Strategic groups** | Groups within the same industry | Budget hotel price plus selected premium services |
| 3. **Chain of buyers** | Purchasers vs. users vs. influencers | Pharma shifting focus from prescribing doctors to patients |
| 4. **Complementary products and services** | The total solution | EV maker providing charging infrastructure |
| 5. **Functional–emotional appeal** | Reversing the industry's orientation | A functional product made into a lifestyle item |
| 6. **Time (external trends)** | Trends that will change value | Redesigning around environmental concern |

**Objective:** find a new value–cost frontier.

### 3.10 TO-BE canvas and the litmus test `[PN][SD]`
- **TO-BE canvas:** the proposed new value curve after applying ERRC. It doesn't need to be higher on every factor, just **different**.
- **Litmus test:**
  1. **Focus:** concentrates on a well-defined, untapped segment or need.
  2. **Divergence:** not imitative of competitors' curves.
  3. **Compelling tagline:** a clear, sharp value proposition.

## 4. Cases

### Case 5 — Nintendo Wii (required) `[PN][SD]`
**Context (2006):**
- Sony PlayStation and Microsoft Xbox competed on processing power, high-definition graphics, online play and huge game libraries.
- The core audience was young male "core gamers".
- It was a red ocean.

| Action | Nintendo Wii |
|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | High-resolution graphics, non-gaming functions, HD capability |
| **Reduce** | Processing power, online gaming, price |
| **Raise** | Design aesthetics, availability of (accessible) titles |
| **Create** | Motion-based controls, family-friendly and social games |

**Results** `[PN]`: from December 2006 to April 2011, cumulative sales were Wii about 86.2m, Xbox 360 about 53.5m, PS3 about 50.1m.

**Lesson:** Nintendo changed the basis of competition and expanded demand through noncustomers (families, casual players, older adults). Note that Nintendo also appears as a mature-industry "new segments" example in S8.

**Optional case IV: Sony PlayStation.** This is the red-ocean contrast: competing on technical specifications. `[REC]`

### Yellow Tail (lecture example) `[PN]`
- **Context:** US wine in 2000 was crowded, demand was stagnant, and products competed on prestige, complexity, ageing and technical jargon. Many people found wine intimidating.
- **ERRC:**
  - **Eliminate** enological jargon, ageing qualities and above-the-line marketing.
  - **Reduce** complexity, range and vineyard prestige.
  - **Raise** price versus budget wines and retail-store involvement.
  - **Create** easy drinking, ease of selection, fun and adventure.
- **Growth:** about 112k cases in 2001 to about 7.5m in 2005; leading imported wine in the US.
- **Lesson:** no new technology is needed; the firm redefined the customer experience.

### Tesla (lecture example) `[PN]`
- **ERRC:**
  - **Eliminate** petrol, tailpipe emissions and engine maintenance.
  - **Reduce** options and servicing.
  - **Raise** efficiency, acceleration and range.
  - **Create** stylish high-performance EVs and an integrated charging network.
- Tesla made EVs *desirable*, not just "green".
- **Challenges:** scaling, cost, infrastructure, competition.

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Red ocean: beat competitors | Blue ocean: make competition irrelevant |
| Porter: choose cost **or** differentiation | Value innovation: cost **and** differentiation |
| AS-IS canvas: the present | TO-BE canvas: the proposed future |
| Four actions: the questions | ERRC grid: the decisions |
| Buyer utility map: improve the buyer experience | Three tiers: find new buyers |
| Tier 1: about to leave | Tier 2: refuses · Tier 3: never considered |
| Eliminate (remove entirely) | Reduce (below standard) |

## 7. Exam traps
- Blue Ocean is **not** about technology or heavy R&D investment.
- The new value curve doesn't have to be **higher** everywhere, just **divergent**.
- Tier 1 noncustomers are *inside or at the edge* of the market; tier 3 are the *farthest*.
- Path 1 = **alternative industries** (different industries, same need); path 2 = **strategic groups** within one industry.
- Blue oceans are **not permanent**: imitation eventually turns them red.
- The litmus test is **focus, divergence and tagline**. It is not "cost, value, profit".

## 9. Memorise this
- **Value innovation = differentiation + low cost** (break the trade-off).
- **ERRC:** eliminate & reduce → cost; raise & create → value.
- **Canvas:** x = competing factors; y = offering level; the value curve is the firm's profile.
- **Tiers:** soon-to-be · refusing · unexplored.
- **BUM:** 6 stages (purchase → disposal) × 6 levers (productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk, fun & image, environment).
- **Six paths:** alternative industries · strategic groups · buyer chain · complements · functional/emotional · time.
- **Litmus test:** focus · divergence · tagline.
- **Cases:** Cirque (circus + theatre) · Wii (gaming + accessibility) · Yellow Tail (wine + simplicity) · Tesla (EV + performance).
