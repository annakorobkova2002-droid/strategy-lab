# S14 — From Strategy to Business Model

## 1. Learning objectives
- Distinguish strategy from the business model, and say what a business model is *not*.
- Compare the traditional planning approach with the lean startup approach.
- Describe and connect the nine building blocks of the Business Model Canvas.
- Distinguish revenue models from pricing models.
- Analyse real business models (Disney, Airbnb, Gillette, AWS).

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Strategy** | How a firm outperforms competitors (and itself over time), measured by profitability. It is about **competition**. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Business model** | The rationale of how an organisation **creates, delivers and captures value**. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Lean startup** (Steve Blank) | Favours experimentation over elaborate planning, customer feedback over intuition, and iterative design over "big decision up front". `[PN][SD]` |
| **Business Model Canvas** (Osterwalder) | A visual tool of nine interrelated building blocks to describe, analyse and design a business model. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Product–market fit** | Coherence between the value proposition and the customer segment. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Revenue model** | *How* money is earned from each segment. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Pricing model** | *How* the price is set for each segment. `[PN][SD]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Strategy vs. business model `[PN][SD]`
- **Create value:** which needs are addressed (needs change over time).
- **Deliver value:** how the target is reached.
- **Capture value:** what share returns to the firm, through the price.

**A business model is NOT** `[SD]`:
- a strategy;
- a technology;
- a product or process innovation;
- a value chain (which is about activities);
- an operational model (which describes the supply-to-distribution process and is *part of* the business model).

**Key warning:** business models are fairly transparent and therefore **imitable**. A successful business model alone does **not** guarantee competitive advantage. Strategy analysis is needed to protect it through **isolating mechanisms** against imitation and disintermediation.

**Coupling strategy with business-model design** `[SD]`:
1. Segment the market.
2. Create a value proposition for each segment.
3. Set up the apparatus to deliver it.
4. Build isolating mechanisms.

**The pathway** `[SD]`: business idea → business model → business plan → financials.

### 3.2 Traditional vs. lean startup `[PN][SD]`
- **Traditional:** idea → business plan → investment → product development → launch.
- **Lean startup:** build–test–learn loops that test assumptions with customers before committing big resources. The aim is to **reduce uncertainty**.

### 3.3 The Business Model Canvas `[PN][SD]`
**Two features** `[SD]`:
- design follows a **sequence**;
- the blocks are **interrelated**: one strong block cannot compensate for one fatal block.

The right side is about **value** and revenue; the left side is about **efficiency** and costs.

| # | Block | Key question | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Value proposition** | What need or "job" do we satisfy? | The customer "hires" the product to do a job |
| 2 | **Customer segments** | Who? How many? How often? | Each segment may need its own value proposition. VP + segment = **product–market fit** |
| 3 | **Channels** | How do we reach customers? | Phases: awareness → evaluation → purchase → delivery → after-sales |
| 4 | **Customer relationships** | How do we **get, keep and grow** customers? | From low to high touch: self-service → automated service → personal assistance → dedicated personal assistance → communities and co-creation `[SD]` |
| 5 | **Revenue streams** | How does each segment pay? | Timing; one-off vs. recurring |
| 6 | **Key resources** | Which assets are essential? | Physical · financial · human · intellectual |
| 7 | **Key activities** | What must we do? | Production, problem-solving, platform/network, supply chain; **make or buy** |
| 8 | **Key partnerships** | Who helps us? | Suppliers, distributors, JVs. Examples: Amazon–logistics partners, Apple–TSMC; partners can be seasonal `[SD]` |
| 9 | **Cost structure** | What are the main costs? | Driven by blocks 6–8. Airlines collect cash *before* incurring costs; shipbuilders incur costs *before* cash `[SD]` |

The "cereal company adding caviar" example `[SD]`: an idea that triggers red flags on the cost side (resources, partners, activities, costs) must be offset by enough value on the right side.

### 3.4 Revenue models `[PN][SD]`
| Model | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Asset sale | Sale of ownership | Products |
| Usage fee | Pay according to use | Pay-per-use, utilities |
| Subscription | Recurring fee for continuous access | Netflix, SaaS |
| Renting / leasing | Temporary access | Car rental |
| Licensing | Fee for using IP | Disney characters |
| Intermediation / brokerage fee | Commission for matching parties | Airbnb |
| Advertising | Advertisers pay for audience | Facebook |
| Freemium | Free basic service, paid premium | LinkedIn, Spotify |

The "cow exercise" `[SD]` shows many revenue streams from one asset: sell the cow, sell milk, sell meat, breed, milk subscriptions, rental, rides, marketing.

### 3.5 Pricing models `[PN][SD]`
| Model | Logic |
|---|---|
| Cost-based | Cost plus a mark-up |
| Value-based | Price set on perceived customer value |
| Competitive | Price set relative to rivals |
| Volume | Discounts for larger quantities |
| Razor / razor-blade | Cheap base product, profitable recurring purchases (Gillette, Nespresso) |
| Portfolio | Different mark-ups across products |

**Revenue model = how money comes in; pricing model = how much is charged.**

### 3.6 Business model examples `[PN][SD]`
| Company | Business model | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Disney** | Scalable, brand- and IP-based | Characters and stories → films (distribution fees) → **licensing** → theme parks → merchandise. One asset, many revenue streams |
| **Facebook** | Multi-sided platform | Free for users; advertisers pay |
| **Airbnb** | Platform | Two value propositions (hosts: an easy way to find renters; travellers: a customised, private stay); transaction fees |
| **Gillette** | Razor / blade | Inexpensive razor, recurring blades |
| **Zara** | Speed / "no-frills" efficiency | Standardised, fast, efficiency-oriented operations |
| **McDonald's** | Franchising | Franchisees run restaurants and pay fees and royalties |

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Strategy: how to beat competitors | Business model: how value is created, delivered and captured |
| Revenue model: how money is earned | Pricing model: how prices are set |
| Business plan: lean startup's opposite (plan first) | Lean startup: test first |
| Right side of the BMC: value and revenue | Left side: efficiency and costs |
| Channels: how we reach customers | Customer relationships: how we get, keep and grow them |
| Make (in-house) | Buy (outsource) |

## 7. Exam traps
- A great business model ≠ competitive advantage: business models are imitable without isolating mechanisms.
- Subscription and freemium are **revenue** models; razor-blade is a **pricing** model. MCQs mix these up.
- The value chain is **not** the business model.
- **Customer relationships** cover *acquisition, retention and growth*, not just after-sales service.
- Product–market fit links **two** BMC blocks: the value proposition and customer segments.

## 9. Memorise this
- **Business model = create + deliver + capture value. Strategy = beat competitors.**
- **BMC (9):** VP · segments · channels · relationships · revenue · resources · activities · partners · costs.
- **Channels:** awareness → evaluation → purchase → delivery → after-sales. **Relationships:** get → keep → grow.
- **Revenue models:** asset sale · usage · subscription · rent · licensing · intermediation · advertising · freemium.
- **Pricing:** cost · value · competitive · volume · razor-blade · portfolio.
- **Disney = one brand → many revenue streams (scalable).**
- **AWS = pay-as-you-go cloud + scale + customer obsession.**
