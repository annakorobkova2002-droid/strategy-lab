# S6 — Competitive Advantage I: Cost Advantage

## 1. Learning objectives
- Define competitive advantage and identify its external and internal sources.
- Explain the seven drivers of cost advantage.
- Distinguish economies of scale, learning, scope and network.
- Use the value chain to analyse a firm's cost position (the five steps).
- Explain the main cost-reduction approaches and apply them to low-cost airlines.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Competitive advantage** | When two or more firms compete in the same market, one has a competitive advantage if it earns, or has the potential to earn, a **persistently higher rate of profit**. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Cost advantage** | Supplying a similar product at a **lower cost** than competitors. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Economies of scale** | Average unit cost falls as **current** output (per period) increases. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Minimum efficient plant size (MES)** | The output level at which most economies of scale have been exhausted. `[PN]` |
| **Economies of learning / experience curve** | Unit cost falls as **cumulative** output increases. The unit cost of value added falls by a roughly constant percentage each time cumulative output doubles; the lecture cites a typical 20–30%. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Economies of scope** | Cost savings from sharing resources and activities across different products or businesses. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Network economies** | The benefit to each user rises as the number of users rises. This is a **demand-side** effect. `[PN]` |
| **Business process reengineering (BPR)** | The fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in performance. `[SD]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Sources of competitive advantage `[PN][SD]`
- **External sources:** changes in customer demand, in prices, in technology. These create advantage for firms with the **capability to anticipate change and respond quickly**.
- **Internal sources:** creative and innovative capabilities, in the form of product/service innovation, process innovation and business model innovation.

**Two types of advantage:** cost advantage (similar product, lower cost) and differentiation advantage (a unique product for which customers pay a premium; see S7).

### 3.2 Cost advantage ≠ low price `[PN]`
A low-cost firm can either:
- **keep market prices** and earn higher margins; or
- **cut prices** to win market share.

Example from the lecture: both firms sell at €100. The rival's unit cost is €80 (profit €20). The low-cost firm's unit cost is €60 (profit €40), with no price reduction.

### 3.3 The seven drivers of cost advantage `[PN][SD]`
| Driver | Sub-drivers |
|---|---|
| 1. **Economies of scale** | Technical input–output relationships, indivisibilities, specialisation |
| 2. **Economies of learning** | Better individual skills, improved organisational routines |
| 3. **Production techniques** | Process innovation, reengineering of business processes |
| 4. **Product design** | Standardised designs and components, design for manufacture |
| 5. **Input costs** | Location advantages, ownership of low-cost inputs, non-union labour, bargaining power |
| 6. **Capacity utilisation** | Ratio of fixed to variable costs, fast and flexible capacity adjustment |
| 7. **Residual efficiency** | Organisational slack / X-inefficiency, motivation and culture, managerial effectiveness |

### 3.4 Economies of scale in more depth `[PN][SD]`
- Sources:
  - **technical input–output relationships** (for example, a tank's capacity grows faster than its surface);
  - **indivisibilities** (some inputs come only in large units);
  - **specialisation** of labour and equipment.
- They are often **larger in R&D, marketing and distribution than in production**. Larger firms also benefit from brand use across locations, risk spreading, cheaper capital and talent attraction.
- **Economies of advertising:** Coca-Cola and Pepsi spread huge advertising budgets over enormous volumes, so the cost per unit is low. `[PN]`

### 3.5 Economies of learning `[PN][SD]`
Sources: better employee skills, better selection of resources, more efficient coordination, more programmable processes, simplified products and processes.

### 3.6 Scope and network economies `[PN][SD]`
- **Scope in production:** shared operations and technology. **Scope in marketing:** common customers, channels and brand.
- **Network economies** are a demand-side benefit. Unlike scale economies, they do not directly lower production cost.

### 3.7 Analysing cost position with the value chain `[PN][SD]`
**Five steps:**
1. Identify the main value-chain activities.
2. Allocate total costs to each activity.
3. Identify the **cost drivers** of each activity.
4. Identify **linkages** between activities. `[SD]` examples:
   - consolidating purchase orders lowers prices but raises inventories;
   - higher-quality parts reduce defect costs later;
   - fewer manufacturing defects cut warranty costs;
   - designing models around common platforms reduces manufacturing costs.
5. Recommend cost reductions.

Cutting cost in one activity can raise or lower costs elsewhere.

### 3.8 Approaches to cost reduction `[PN][SD]`
| Approach | Key elements |
|---|---|
| **Corporate restructuring** | Plant closures, outsourcing, delayering, cuts in administrative staff |
| **Business process reengineering** | Combining several jobs into one, natural order of process steps, fewer steps/controls/reconciliations, case managers as single points of contact, hybrid centralisation/decentralisation |
| **Shared-service centres** | Centralising common functions (HR, finance, IT, payroll, legal, purchasing, customer service). Benefits: standardised processes, efficiency, control, unified IT |

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale: **current** output | Economies of learning: **cumulative** output |
| Economies of scope: sharing across **products** | Network economies: value to users rises with **users** (demand side) |
| Cost advantage: lower **cost** | Low price: a pricing *choice* that may or may not follow |
| Restructuring: changes structure | BPR: redesigns processes |

## 7. Case 3 — Low-cost airlines `[PN]` + `[REC]`
**How the model creates cost advantage** `[PN]`:
| Element | Cost effect |
|---|---|
| **Unbundling** | Passengers pay separately for extras (bags, seats, food), which lowers the base cost and adds revenue |
| **Single aircraft type** | Simpler training, maintenance and spare parts |
| **Secondary airports** | Lower airport charges, less congestion |
| **Fast turnaround, high aircraft utilisation** | More flights per aircraft per day (capacity utilisation) |
| **Simplified service** | Lower operating costs |
| **Efficient maintenance** | Less complexity and downtime |
| Tax benefits and salary costs | Also raised in class |

**Southwest vs. United** `[PN]`:
- In the historical cost-per-seat-mile comparison shown in the lecture, Southwest had **lower total cost** even though some activities, such as maintenance, cost more.

**Linking to the seven drivers** `[REC]`:
- Product design: a standardised fleet.
- Capacity utilisation: fast turnaround.
- Input costs: secondary airports, labour terms.
- Economies of learning: one aircraft type.
- Residual efficiency: a lean culture.
- Examples: Ryanair, easyJet, Southwest. The 2024–25 notes used **AirAsia**. `[SD]`

**Lesson:** cost advantage comes from an **integrated system of mutually reinforcing activities**, not from being cheapest in every single activity. That system is also hard to imitate (link to VRIO in S5).

## 8. Practical application
Template for a cost question:
1. Define cost advantage.
2. Identify which of the seven drivers the firm uses.
3. Show the activity linkages.
4. Explain the effect on margin or share.
5. Assess sustainability: can rivals imitate the system?

## 9. Exam traps
- **Cost advantage does not require lower prices.**
- Scale relates to *current* output, learning to *cumulative* output. This is the most common MCQ trap.
- Network economies are **not** a supply-side cost driver.
- In the Southwest comparison, the winner was not cheapest in *every* activity.
- MES is where scale economies are *exhausted*, not where they start.

## 11. Memorise this
- **CA = persistently higher profit (or potential to earn it).**
- **7 drivers:** scale · learning · production techniques · product design · input costs · capacity utilisation · residual efficiency.
- **Scale = current; learning = cumulative (20–30% per doubling); scope = across products; network = demand side.**
- **5 steps:** activities → allocate costs → cost drivers → linkages → recommend.
- **Cost reduction:** restructuring · BPR · shared-service centres.
- **Low-cost airlines = a reinforcing system** (unbundling, one fleet, secondary airports, fast turnaround).
