# S12–S13 — Global and Multidomestic Industries and Strategies

## 1. Learning objectives
- Explain the five strategic decisions of internationalisation, and how globalisation is changing.
- Explain why firms internationalise (and the risks), and the sources of international competitive advantage.
- Use CAGE and the timing-of-entry matrix to choose and sequence markets.
- Compare foreign entry modes.
- Classify international industries and choose between standardisation and customisation.
- Explain value-chain location choices and the organisational models of multinationals.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Internationalisation** | A firm expanding its operations, sales or investments into foreign countries. `[PN]` |
| **Globalisation** | The growing economic, political and social interdependence between countries, firms and people. `[PN]` |
| **CAGE** | A framework measuring the **C**ultural, **A**dministrative, **G**eographic and **E**conomic distance between countries. `[PN]` |
| **FDI** | Foreign direct investment: ownership and control of business activities abroad. `[PN]` |
| **Licensing** | Granting the right to use intellectual property for a fee or royalties. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Franchising** | Granting the right to operate using an established brand, business model or process for fees and royalties. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Joint venture** | A separate entity jointly owned by two or more partners that pool resources. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Greenfield** | Setting up new foreign operations from scratch. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Offshoring** | Moving an activity to a foreign country (a question of WHERE). `[PN]` |
| **Outsourcing** | Moving an activity to an external provider (a question of WHO). `[PN]` |
| **Transnational corporation** | An integrated network of interdependent subsidiaries that share knowledge and resources. `[PN][SD]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Five decisions of internationalisation `[PN]`
1. **Why** go abroad? (internationalisation)
2. **Where?** (location strategy)
3. **How?** (entry mode)
4. **Standardise or adapt?** (international strategy)
5. **How to manage it?** (organisational choices)

### 3.2 Globalisation and its slowdown `[PN]`
**Drivers:**
- technology;
- trade liberalisation;
- better transport and communication;
- financial integration;
- differences in cost and resources;
- international R&D.

**Recent slowdown:**
- geopolitical tensions and tariffs;
- COVID supply-chain shocks;
- economic nationalism;
- wars;
- a priority on supply-chain **resilience** over pure cost;
- sustainability concerns;
- automation and AI reducing the value of cheap labour.

**New forms of globalisation:**
- **Slowbalisation:** slower integration.
- **Regionalisation:** trade within regional blocs.
- **Friend-shoring:** moving activities to politically aligned countries.
- **Selective globalisation:** integration continues except in strategic sectors.

Globalisation is being **reorganised**, not abandoned.

### 3.3 Why go abroad, and the risks `[PN][SD]`
- **Revenue motives:**
  - market expansion;
  - protecting revenue when the home market is mature or declining;
  - **following global customers** (B2B);
  - reputation;
  - knowledge acquisition.
- **Cost motives:**
  - economies of scale when minimum efficient scale exceeds the home market;
  - exploiting existing resources in more markets;
  - cheaper inputs.
- **Competitive motive** `[SD]`: international presence as a competitive benchmark.

**Risks:**
| Risk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic | Market volatility, demand fluctuations |
| Exchange-rate | Currency fluctuations and devaluation |
| Technical | Product requirements, local standards and compliance |
| Country | Political instability, bureaucracy, taxes, trade barriers |

### 3.4 Competitive advantage in an international context `[PN][SD]`
Advantage comes from three sources:
- **firm resources and capabilities**;
- the **industry environment** (KSFs);
- the **national environment**: resources, infrastructure, institutions, policy, exchange rates, supporting industries.

**Comparative advantage** `[SD]`: a country is relatively efficient in products that use its abundant resources intensively. Examples: the Philippines in footwear and assembled electronics; the US in semiconductors and pharmaceuticals. With well-behaved exchange rates, comparative advantage becomes competitive advantage.

**Porter's National Diamond** `[SD]` (named) `[TB]` (content):
1. **Factor conditions:** advanced, specialised factors.
2. **Demand conditions:** sophisticated home buyers.
3. **Related and supporting industries:** clusters.
4. **Firm strategy, structure and rivalry:** strong domestic competition.

These four explain why some nations host world-class industries (for example, Italian fashion and design).

### 3.5 Location strategy and CAGE `[PN][SD]`
**Location criteria:**
- **location characteristics:** institutions, resources, clusters;
- **government policies:** FDI rules, tax incentives, hostility or support;
- **fit with the firm:** distance, strategic compatibility, partners;
- **competitive effect:** input costs and product competitiveness.

**CAGE** (Ghemawat) `[PN]`:
| Distance | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Cultural** | Language, religion, traditions, social norms |
| **Administrative** | Regulation, trade agreements, political relations, colonial ties |
| **Geographic** | Physical distance, borders, transport and infrastructure |
| **Economic** | Income levels, labour costs, purchasing power |

**Sensitivity varies by industry** `[PN]`:
- **Agrifood:** high cultural distance, medium on the other three.
- **Fashion:** high cultural, low administrative and geographic, medium economic.
- **Fintech:** low cultural, high administrative, low geographic, high economic.

Example: the **McDonald's** Masala McEgg in India (cultural adaptation). **Frontier economies** `[SD]` offer high growth but weak institutions.

### 3.6 Timing of entry `[PN]`
| | **High distance** | **Low distance** |
|---|---|---|
| **High strategic importance** | **Phased-in entry** (Starbucks in Italy) | **Rapid entry** (Google US → Europe) |
| **Low strategic importance** | **Ignore for now** (Campari in Indonesia) | **Opportunistic entry** (Italian TV broadcasters in Albania) |

Early entry means first-mover advantages but more uncertainty; late entry means learning from others but fewer opportunities.

**Sequence:** CAGE → distance → strategic importance → entry timing → investment commitment.

### 3.7 Entry modes `[PN][SD]`
**The five key questions:**
1. Is the advantage firm-specific or country-specific?
2. Is the product tradable, and what are the trade barriers?
3. Does the firm have all the resources needed abroad?
4. Can it appropriate the returns to its resources?
5. What are the transaction costs?

**Continuum of increasing commitment and control:**
| Mode | Equity? | Commitment | Notes and examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exporting** (spot sales, long-term contracts, distributors) | No | Low | Low investment; dependence on distributors, transport costs, trade barriers. Barilla uses foreign distributors. |
| **Licensing** | No | Low–moderate | Fee plus royalties; usually around 5 years; exclusive or non-exclusive `[SD]`. Ferrari licensing to apparel makers. |
| **Franchising** | No | Low–moderate | See the three types below. McDonald's. |
| **Strategic alliance** `[SD]` | Usually no | Moderate | Each partner stays independent. **Upstream** (supply, R&D), **manufacturing**, **downstream** (marketing, distribution). |
| **Joint venture** | Shared | Moderate–high | **Horizontal** (same industry, often competitors) vs. **vertical** (different value-chain stages, complementary). **Kellogg–Tolaram** (Africa). |
| **Minority investment** | < 50% | Moderate | Local knowledge with lower commitment, but no control. **Mastercard → Jumia.** |
| **Acquisition / M&A** | > 50% | High | Fast entry, existing capabilities; integration risk. **Campari** (Grand Marnier, Picon). |
| **Greenfield** | 100% | Highest | Full control and design; slow, costly. **Starbucks Reserve Roastery** Milan, **Shanghai Disneyland**. |

**Three types of franchising** `[PN][SD]`:
| Type | Franchisee does… | Pros / cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Business format** | Runs the whole established business | + brand, training, proven model, customers · − little independence, quality control, narrow margins |
| **Product / distribution** | Sells the manufacturer's branded products | + brand, low upfront cost, autonomy · − little support, needs its own store |
| **Manufacturing** | Produces with the franchisor's technology and brand | + production autonomy · − high start-up costs, strict standards, raw-material exposure |

**Coca-Cola in Africa** `[PN][SD]`: local bottlers buy concentrate, produce, bottle and distribute, partly through "manual distribution centres".

**Kellogg–Tolaram** `[PN][SD]`:
- Kellogg brought brands and food capabilities; Tolaram (maker of Indomie noodles) brought local knowledge and distribution.
- They launched a Kellogg noodles range (South Africa, 2017).
- They built a noodle factory in **Eswatini** with government co-investment.
- Kellogg also bought **50% of Multipro** (Nigerian distribution).

**Greenfield vs. M&A:** greenfield is new, slower and fully designed by the firm; M&A is faster, buys existing capabilities, but brings integration and culture challenges.

### 3.8 Types of international industries `[PN]`
| | **Low FDI** | **High FDI** |
|---|---|---|
| **High trade** | **International (trading) industries:** shipbuilding, diamond mining, agriculture | **Global industries:** autos, oil & gas, semiconductors |
| **Low trade** | **Sheltered industries:** hairdressing, laundries, fresh milk | **Multidomestic industries:** hotels, retail banking, food chains |

**Global industries** are driven by scale, transport and technology; **multidomestic industries** by local needs and tastes, diseconomies of scale and complexity. `[SD]`

### 3.9 Standardisation vs. customisation `[PN][SD]`
| Customisation | Standardisation |
|---|---|
| Adapt to each national market | One product worldwide |
| Local responsiveness | Global efficiency and scale |
| Higher adaptation costs, fragmented logistics | Common technology, coordinated logistics |
| Multidomestic and sheltered industries | Global and international industries |

**Three approaches** `[SD]`:
1. The world as a **single market** (same product everywhere).
2. The world as **interrelated regional markets** (EMEA, LATAM, Asia).
3. The world as **separate national markets** (a different product per country).

Firms often **combine** the approaches, with a global platform plus local adaptation.

### 3.10 Value-chain location: where and who? `[PN][SD]`
| Location \ Actor | Keep in-house | Outsource | Insource |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Home country** | Status quo | Outsourced backshoring | In-house backshoring |
| **Foreign country** | Offshoring | Outsourced offshoring | In-house offshoring / nearshoring |

- **Offshoring = WHERE; outsourcing = WHO.** A firm can offshore without outsourcing.
- **Location factors:**
  - national resources and costs;
  - how specific and transferable the firm's advantage is;
  - tradability and transport costs.

Example: design kept in Italy, production abroad.

**Control matrix** `[PN]`:
| Need for direct control | Production | Sales |
|---|---|---|
| High | Owned production subsidiary | Owned trading subsidiary |
| Medium–high | Production JV | Trading JV |
| Medium–low | Outsourced local assembly | Licensing / franchising |
| Low | Local outsourcing | Exporting |

**Boeing 787 Dreamliner:**
- wings from Japan;
- fuselage and stabiliser parts from Italy;
- engines and equipment from the UK;
- landing gear from France;
- final assembly in the US.

This is a globally dispersed production network.

### 3.11 Organising the multinational `[PN][SD]`
| Model | Subsidiary autonomy | HQ role | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **European decentralised federation** | High | General oversight, best-practice sharing | Unilever, Philips, Royal Dutch Shell |
| **American coordinated federation** | Moderate–high (operational) | Controls technology, products, resources and cash flows; judges subsidiaries on financial results | Ford, GM, Coca-Cola, IBM |
| **Japanese centralised hub** | Low (sales and distribution only) | Sets strategy, technology and production | Honda, Toyota, NEC |
| **Transnational** | Interdependent network | Coordinates collaboration; each unit is a source of ideas and a global centre for specific products | Nestlé, Unilever, P&G `[SD]` |

## 4. Case 6 — Domino's Pizza (required) `[PN]` + `[REC]`
**India** `[PN]`:
- Domino's adapted its **menu** to local tastes (vegetarian and local flavours).
- It kept a **standardised, efficient delivery system**, **digital ordering** and competitive pricing.
- It entered through a **local master-franchise partner** (Jubilant FoodWorks `[REC]`).

**Theory:**
- Fast food is a **multidomestic** industry. Domino's combines **customisation** (product) with **standardisation** (processes and technology); this is the "combined" approach.
- **Entry mode:** business-format franchising, which uses local capabilities with low capital.
- **CAGE:** cultural distance (food habits) and economic distance (price sensitivity) drove the adaptations.

**Lesson:** international success needs local adaptation **and** operational efficiency.

## 6. Exam traps
- **Offshoring ≠ outsourcing.**
- **Multidomestic** = low trade, high FDI (local presence needed). **International** = high trade, low FDI.
- **Licensing** transfers specific IP; **franchising** transfers a whole business system.
- A **minority investment** buys a stake in an *existing* firm; a **JV** creates a *new* jointly owned entity.
- **CAGE:** geography is only one distance of four, and is often not the most important.
- **Japanese hub** = centralised; **European federation** = decentralised (don't swap them).

## 8. Memorise this
- **5 decisions:** why · where · how · adapt? · organise.
- **CAGE:** cultural · administrative · geographic · economic.
- **Timing:** rapid · phased-in · opportunistic · ignore (importance × distance).
- **Entry modes:** export → licensing/franchising → alliance → JV → minority stake → M&A → greenfield (rising control).
- **Franchising:** business format · product/distribution · manufacturing.
- **Industries:** sheltered · international · multidomestic · global (trade × FDI).
- **Offshoring = WHERE; outsourcing = WHO.**
- **MNC models:** European decentralised · American coordinated · Japanese centralised · transnational.
- **Domino's India = local menu + standard delivery tech + franchise partner.**
