# S2–S3 — Analysing Industry Attractiveness

## 1. Learning objectives
- Explain why industry structure drives competition and profitability.
- Use PESTEL to scan the macro-environment.
- Apply Porter's Five Forces and explain each force's determinants.
- Define an industry using substitutability and identify Key Success Factors (KSFs).
- Recognise the limits of Five Forces analysis, including complements.

## 2. Core definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Industry analysis** | The study of how industry structure shapes competition and therefore industry profitability. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Industry attractiveness** | An industry's potential to earn high average profits. It is high when the competitive forces are weak. `[PN]` |
| **Macro-environment** | General external influences (political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal) that affect the firm *indirectly*, through their effect on the industry. `[SD]` |
| **Industry environment** | Suppliers, competitors and customers: the firm's *direct* environment. `[SD]` |
| **Substitute** | A different product or service that satisfies a similar need, even if it is different in nature. It *replaces* value. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Complement** | A product or service that *increases* the value of another product. `[PN]` |
| **Barrier to entry** | Any advantage incumbents have over potential entrants that makes entry harder. `[PN][SD]` |
| **Key Success Factor (KSF)** | What a firm must do well to survive and prosper in an industry. `[PN]` |

## 3. Concepts explained

### 3.1 Objectives of industry analysis `[SD][PN]`
1. Understand how industry structure drives competition and thus profitability.
2. Assess industry attractiveness (a corporate-strategy question).
3. Forecast future profitability from changes in structure.
4. Identify KSFs (a business-strategy question).
5. Formulate strategies that change industry structure to improve profitability.

### 3.2 PESTEL `[PN][SD]`
**P**olitical · **E**conomic · **S**ocial · **T**echnological · **E**nvironmental · **L**egal.

**Process:** research trends → identify interconnections → forecast directions → judge positive or negative impact → derive strategic implications. The output can be drawn as an **impact map**.

PESTEL is a filter. It helps managers separate the *vital* influences from the *merely important* ones.

### 3.3 Three determinants of industry profitability `[SD]`
1. The **value** of the product to customers.
2. The **intensity of competition**.
3. **Relative bargaining power** at different levels of the value chain.

Porter's Five Forces turn these three ideas into a structured tool.

### 3.4 Porter's Five Forces
**General rule:** strong forces → low attractiveness → low profitability. `[PN]`

**A. Threat of new entrants.** The threat depends on the height of entry barriers:
- **Capital requirements.**
- **Economies of scale.**
- **Absolute cost advantages:** learning, proprietary technology, favourable locations, subsidies.
- **Product differentiation:** brand loyalty.
- **Access to distribution channels.**
- **Legal and regulatory barriers.**

Higher barriers → lower threat → higher profitability. `[PN][SD]`

**B. Threat of substitutes.** The threat depends on two things:
- Buyers' **propensity to substitute**.
- The **price–performance** of the substitutes.

Example: camera phones displaced mid-range cameras. `[SD]`

**C. Bargaining power of buyers.** Two components `[SD]`:
- **Price sensitivity:**
  - the cost of the item as a share of the buyer's total costs;
  - how differentiated the item is;
  - how intense competition is among buyers;
  - how important the item is to the quality of the buyer's own output.
- **Relative bargaining power:**
  - buyers' size and concentration relative to sellers;
  - buyers' information;
  - buyers' ability to integrate backward.

Buyer power rises with `[PN][SD]`:
- large purchase volumes;
- undifferentiated products;
- low switching costs;
- good buyer information;
- a credible threat of backward integration;
- low buyer profits.

Example: Walmart moving into milk processing hurt Dean Foods' share price. `[SD]`

**D. Bargaining power of suppliers.** Supplier power rises with `[PN][SD]`:
- few suppliers relative to buyers (supplier concentration);
- unique or differentiated inputs;
- high switching costs;
- no substitute inputs;
- a credible threat of forward integration;
- an input that strongly affects the buyer's cost or quality.

Example: Intel chips. `[SD]`

**E. Rivalry among existing competitors.** Rivalry rises with `[PN][SD]`:
- **concentration** (the number and size distribution of firms): many similar-sized rivals mean more rivalry;
- high **diversity** of competitors;
- low product **differentiation** and low switching costs;
- high **fixed costs** relative to variable costs;
- **excess capacity**;
- high **exit barriers**;
- **low industry growth**;
- **perishable** products.

High rivalry → price competition → lower profitability. Industries such as cars, mining and hotels show this pattern. `[SD]`

> ⚠ **Concentration and rivalry.** The Studocu notes list concentration with a "+" without saying which way it works. Grant's principle is that *lower* concentration (many similar-sized sellers) usually means *more* intense rivalry, while a market dominated by one leader often has less. `[TB]`

### 3.5 Defining the industry `[PN][SD]`
The key criterion is **substitutability**:
- **Demand side:** are customers willing to switch between products?
- **Supply side:** can producers switch production between products?

Boundaries are seldom clear-cut, and they depend on the *purpose* of the analysis.

Example: is Jaguar in the motor-vehicle industry, the automobile industry or the luxury-car industry? Is that industry global, European or UK-only? `[SD]`

### 3.6 Key Success Factors `[PN]`
Two questions identify KSFs:
- **Demand analysis:** what do customers want?
- **Competition analysis:** how does a firm survive competition?

**Customer needs + competitive requirements → KSFs.**

Example (fashion): differentiation, fast response to trends, style, reputation, quality, cost efficiency.

### 3.7 Using industry analysis `[SD]`
1. Explain differences in profitability between industries and over time.
2. Position the firm where the forces are weakest.
3. Predict changes in competition and profitability.
4. Identify ways to change industry structure (for example, by raising barriers or reducing rivalry).

### 3.8 Limitations of Five Forces `[PN][SD]`
1. **Identifying KSFs** is not automatic.
2. **Missing factors.** **Complements** are the main one: their suppliers' power affects the industry's share of value. Some authors call them the "sixth force".
3. **Uncertainty and rapid structural change** (hypercompetition), though systematic evidence of faster change is elusive.
4. **Choosing the right level of analysis**, which leads into segmentation in S4.

The model is also static and focuses on *industry*, not *firm*, profitability. `[TB]`

## 5. Authors

## 6. Comparisons
| A | B |
|---|---|
| Substitute: replaces value; lowers the industry's prices | Complement: adds value; raises demand |
| Macro-environment (PESTEL): indirect | Industry environment (Five Forces): direct |
| Industry attractiveness → corporate strategy | KSFs → business strategy |
| Demand-side substitutability (customers switch) | Supply-side substitutability (producers switch) |
| Entry barrier (keeps firms out) | Exit barrier (keeps firms in, which *increases* rivalry) |

## 7. Company examples
Camera phones vs. cameras (substitutes) · Walmart and Dean Foods (buyer backward integration) · Intel (supplier power) · Jaguar (industry definition) · fashion (KSFs).

## 8. Case 1 — Beyond Meat `[REC]` ⚠
> The video case was not available. This analysis is reconstructed from widely reported public facts and should be checked against the video.

**Context.**
- Beyond Meat, founded by Ethan Brown in 2009, makes plant-based meat substitutes.
- After a very strong IPO in 2019, it suffered years of falling revenue, losses and price pressure as hype around the category faded.

**Strategic question.** Is the plant-based meat industry attractive?

| Force | Assessment | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| New entrants | **High** | Low technological barriers for large food firms; private labels; big meat incumbents launched their own plant-based lines. |
| Substitutes | **High** | Conventional meat (cheaper, familiar taste), other proteins (tofu, legumes) and hybrid products. |
| Buyer power | **High** | Large retailers and fast-food chains buy in volume, control shelf space and can switch suppliers; consumers are price-sensitive. |
| Supplier power | **Moderate** | Pea protein is a key input with a limited supplier base; co-manufacturing dependence. |
| Rivalry | **High** | Many brands with similar products, price cuts, low switching costs, slowing category growth. |

**Conclusion.**
- An industry that looked like a high-growth opportunity had **weak structural protection**.
- Profitability is squeezed despite innovation.
- KSFs: taste parity, **price parity** with meat, scale (cost), distribution and brand trust.

**Exam lesson.** High *growth* is not the same as high *attractiveness*. Five Forces explain why even a pioneer can struggle to earn profits.

## 9. Exam traps
- Strong forces mean an **unattractive** industry (not the reverse).
- High **exit** barriers increase rivalry. High **entry** barriers reduce the threat of entry.
- A substitute is not the same as a competitor: Coca-Cola vs. Pepsi is rivalry; Coca-Cola vs. bottled water is substitution.
- Complements are **not** one of Porter's original five forces.
- Five Forces assess the *industry*. They do not say which *firm* will win.
- Buyers here means the direct customers of the industry, who may be retailers rather than end consumers.

## 10. Likely open questions
1. **"Apply Five Forces to industry X and assess its attractiveness."** Structure:
   - define the industry (product and geography);
   - assess each force with its drivers (low / medium / high);
   - give an overall verdict;
   - name the KSFs and the implications for a firm.
2. **"What are the limitations of Five Forces?"**
   - Name the four limitations, including complements.
   - Mention that the model is static.
3. **"How can a firm improve industry profitability?"**
   - Raise entry barriers, reduce rivalry (for example through consolidation), reduce buyer power (through differentiation or switching costs).
4. **Beyond Meat:** "Why did a fast-growing pioneer fail to become profitable?"

## 11. Memorise this
- **Strong forces = low profits.**
- **Five forces:** entry · substitutes · buyers · suppliers · rivalry.
- **Entry barriers:** capital · scale · cost advantages · differentiation · distribution · regulation.
- **Industry definition = substitutability** (demand and supply side).
- **KSF = what customers want + how to survive competition.**
- **Limits:** KSFs · missing factors (complements) · change · level of analysis.
